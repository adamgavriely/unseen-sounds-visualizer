Workflow and models3 min

Seven existing models, none retrained, one decision that matters

Say: "Hear the sound, ask a vision model whether its source is already in view, draw a picture only when it isn't."
In simple words: the video goes in at the left. The top row is the main path: take the audio out → find the sounds and when they happen → ask a vision model "is the thing making this sound on screen?" → write a short phrase and draw it → put the pictures in a panel beside the video. The bottom row are helpers: an object finder gives candidates, the speech is written down only as a hint (if people say "what was that?" the sound gets priority), and the last box scores the result. Only sounds that are heard but not seen reach the picture stage.

The one design decision worth 30 seconds — stage 5. The vision model is told which sound was heard and asked what in the frames is making it — "name it, don't confirm it" — because a model asked "do you see it?" agrees. Three differently worded votes per 5-second stretch; a picture is withheld only if the source is visible in every stretch, so errors lean toward showing: for a deaf viewer a redundant picture costs less than a missed siren.

Baselines: blind — a picture for every sound, never looks at the video; caption — the sound's name as text.

What works, what does not1 min

Real test clips, scored by the judge (ours / blind)

Say: "It works when the sound is clear and the source is genuinely out of view; it fails in three ways — the detector never hears a buried sound, the label is debatable, or the picture drawn is the wrong thing."

Works

  • Correct picture, source out of view. Ambulance behind the camera → "ambulance siren blaring" (4 / 3). Basement scene: cattle and a siren both off screen → two pictures (4 / 3).
  • Correct silence, source on screen. Police car with its siren; a London protest; a mountain stream; rain on a road; logging in a forest — panel empty, 4 / 3 on nine such clips: blind draws a redundant picture each time.
  • Less clutter. Panel on 47% of a clip vs 60%; 30% less panel time where nothing was due.
  • Timing. Pictures start where the sound begins (mean error 0.31 s on DCASE's human onsets).

Does not work

  • Buried sound never detected (7 of 11 misses). Cobblestone road: a train and traffic under speech — nothing reached the 0.35 bar, panel empty (0 / 2).
  • Debatable label (4 of 11). New York street (0 / 3); a motorcycle ride where the rider is seen but the label says the engine is "unseen" (0 / 1).
  • Wrong picture drawn. Helicopter clip: the Vehicle sound depicted as a megaphone — judge gives 0 although a helicopter picture was also shown (0 / 3). Storm: Thunder drawn as a bird (1 / 3). Pet shop: an owl at 0.35 drawn as a generic songbird (1 / 3).
  • Phantoms drawn. Sheep in a jungle (0.41), a dog nobody hears (0.60), a whale at a Christmas market (0.36): the gate is right that nothing visible makes them, so the picture appears — about 80% of unnecessary pictures.

The table in simple words: "oracle" is the score a perfect gate would get — one that always knows whether the source is visible (it uses my labels). Both systems are about 0.45 below it. Ours loses on the top row (a picture was due and we withheld it), blind loses on the middle row (no picture was due and it drew one). The bottom row is the tie.
In simple words: the detector gives every sound a confidence between 0 and 1, and we only accept sounds above a bar (0.35 now). Across = how many of the false sounds (things it reports that are not there — "a whale at a Christmas market") we get rid of. Up = how many of the real sounds we keep. The grey line is what happens as we raise the bar step by step: the further right we go (fewer false sounds), the further down we fall (we lose real sounds too). The dots are the other ideas we tried — a second detector that must agree, an audio language model that vetoes, separating voices from the audio — and they all land on or below that same line. Meaning: none of them is a free lunch; each one buys fewer false sounds with lost real sounds, exactly like just raising the bar. The dotted lines are the targets we set before running: remove at least 40 of the 77 false sounds while losing at most 2 of the 23 real ones — nothing reached the top-right corner.

Examples of the four scenarios
  • unseen (picture due): a church bell ringing over a square, the bell out of view; a smoke alarm in a kitchen; a dog barking off-screen while fireworks are lit
  • mixed (picture due): the ambulance clip — siren behind the camera, cars in view; a bridge scene with a helicopter overhead and gunshots

  • no ambient (no picture due): an interview; a music video; a film dialogue scene

Labelling rule (DCMP): wind roaring over a rider filmed on a moving motorcycle is seen — the rider already says it. Split: 100 test clips (25 per scenario) scored once; 174 development clips for settings. Outside check: on DCASE's human labels our visibility decision agrees 66.7% ± 6 — it keeps 97% of off-screen sounds and silences 35% of on-screen ones, so it errs toward showing.

Deaf and hard-of-hearing viewers: what is known, what is not2 min

The literature justifies the design; nothing yet justifies "helps DHH viewers"

What the literature says

  • DHH users most want alarms, sirens, knocks, a baby crying, a dog barking surfaced — HomeSound (Jain et al., CHI 2020), SoundWatch (ASSETS 2020)
  • Users prefer icons and pictures to text labels — SoundWatch
  • Trust collapses after false alarms; users switch the system off — HomeSound
  • Captioners omit a non-speech sound when the picture already shows it — DCMP guidelines
  • Non-speech sounds are inconsistently captioned or dropped — Kushalnagar et al.; Revuelta et al. 2020

What this project did not test

  • No DHH participants at any stage — the proposal specified automatic evaluation
  • The judge (a text model scoring 0–4) is a proxy for a viewer, not a viewer
  • No timing, trust or attention-load measures; the 1.5-s dwell and 3-slot panel are unvalidated choices
  • A muted-clip check with 2–3 hearing people was designed, not run
  • Whether home/wearable findings transfer to watching video is assumed, not shown

Design choices that follow from the literature: pictures rather than text (SoundWatch users chose icons); alarms and sirens matter most (HomeSound users asked for them first); the visibility gate itself (DCMP: omit what the picture already shows) — with the trade-off that false alarms kill trust, which is why the gate exists at all.

Can say: "on 100 clips the pictures are judged correct against a captioner-rule reference, at 30% less wasted panel time than showing everything." Cannot say: "this helps DHH viewers" — no DHH viewer has seen the output.

"Then who is this for?" "Literature only, so far; whether to say so plainly is decision 4, on the right." Future work, two lines: a within-subject study, 20–30 DHH participants, muted test clips, three conditions (gated / blind / caption): sound-event recall, intrusiveness, trust after a seeded false alarm — preceded by the 3-person hearing pilot to validate the protocol.
