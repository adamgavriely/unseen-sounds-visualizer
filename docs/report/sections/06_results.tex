\section{Results}\label{sec:results}
This section reports the final system against the three baselines of the proposal and three one-model baselines, on
158 clips scored per sound against human labels (Table~\ref{tab:sets}). Every comparison is paired by clip with a
100{,}000-draw bootstrap, the five main tests are Holm-corrected, and the cost is re-examined across weights
(Sections~\ref{sec:support} and~\ref{sec:sensitivity}). Every decision was taken on the development set; the test set
was only scored (Section~\ref{sec:passrules}).

\subsection{Baselines}\label{sec:baselines}
The proposal names three baselines. All are run on exactly the same clips and scored with exactly the same rules:
\begin{description}
  \item[Direct audio-to-image generation] is the final system with the on-screen check switched off: the same
        detection stack, listeners, display rules and picture model, with a picture for every surviving sound. The
        other steps that read the video to decide visibility, chiefly the depict-event check, are switched off with
        it (Appendix~\ref{sec:baselinedetail}). Because everything else is held fixed, the comparison isolates the
        question the proposal asks: what reasoning about what the video already shows adds to the same detections.
        (Published audio-to-image models draw a whole scene from the soundtrack and cannot be scored per sound;
        Section~\ref{sec:related}.)
  \item[Audio captioning] describes the same sounds in text instead of pictures. Under the per-sound score a caption is
        right or wrong at exactly the same moments as the audio-to-image picture, so it has the same score; the
        difference between text and pictures can only be measured with viewers (Section~\ref{sec:limits}).
  \item[Show nothing] no augmentation at all: the situation of a viewer with subtitles only.
\end{description}
A generator conditioned on the video and audio that would ``generate what is not seen'' (for example MiniMax-H3) was
also raised during supervision. It produces whole new video, which the per-sound score cannot evaluate, so it was not
run as a baseline (Section~\ref{sec:limits}).

On the test set the final system finds 25 of 66 needed sounds, and more than half of its pictures are right. Drawing every detected
sound finds 28, but only 32\,\% of its pictures are right, and on the test set it is no better than showing nothing:
it costs 0.069 more per clip ($[-0.621, +0.736]$, $p = 0.86$, uncorrected). On the development set it costs less than
showing nothing ($-0.817$, $[-1.577, -0.085]$, $p = 0.032$, uncorrected). The final system is cheaper than both: by 0.713 per clip against direct
audio-to-image generation (23\,\% of that baseline's cost, $p < 0.001$) and by 0.644 against showing nothing
(21\,\%, $p = 0.018$).

On the development set the direction is the same, but the difference from direct audio-to-image is smaller ($-0.394$,
$p = 0.085$, also after Holm). Two things differ between the sets. Direct audio-to-image shows fewer wrong pictures per clip on the
development set (39 over 71 clips, 0.55 per clip, against 59 over 87, 0.68), mostly because it shows fewer other-sound
pictures there (14 against 32). And the check costs more hits there: five fewer than direct audio-to-image on the
development set, but only three fewer on the test set.

The win over direct audio-to-image generation comes from giving up a few hits to remove many wrong pictures. On the
test set the final system has 3 fewer hits and 37 fewer wrong pictures: $(2\cdot 37 - 4\cdot 3)/87 = 0.713$ per clip,
or 12 wrong pictures removed per hit lost. On the development set it has 5 fewer hits and 24 fewer wrong pictures:
$(2\cdot 24 - 4\cdot 5)/71 = 0.394$, or 4.8 per hit lost. Of the 37 wrong pictures removed on the test set, 12 are
on-screen pictures, 17 show another sound and 8 show nothing; on the development set the 24 removed are 14, 7 and 3
(Figure~\ref{fig:wrongkind}). Silencing a sound also removes its late repeat pictures, and the depict-event check,
which is off in the baseline, removes some more. Figure~\ref{fig:breakdown} shows the two effects on the cost.

F1, which weighs a miss and a wrong picture equally, is also higher for the final system on both sets: 0.57 against
0.52 on the development set ($+0.050$, $[-0.039, +0.128]$) and 0.44 against 0.37 on the test set
($+0.076$, $[+0.005, +0.146]$, $p = 0.036$ uncorrected). The intervals use the same clip resampling, with F1 recomputed from the
resampled counts on each draw. F1 favours the final system even though it
values a removed wrong picture no more than a lost hit.

\paragraph{Danger sounds.} The sounds that matter most to a deaf viewer are the importance-3 sounds: danger or a key
moment, such as a siren, an alarm, glass or a baby crying (Section~\ref{sec:needed}). On the test set the final system
keeps every danger sound that drawing every sound finds (10 of 13) while showing 37 fewer wrong pictures; on the
development set it finds 14 of the 17, one fewer (Figure~\ref{fig:importance}). The misses are mostly ordinary events of importance 2.

\subsection{One-model baselines}\label{sec:onemodel}
The baselines above share the final system's detectors. Three more baselines each use one ready-made model and nothing
from this system; a picture is counted for every sound the model reports, except speech and music:
\begin{description}
  \item[M2D-strong] PretrainedSED M2D-strong~\cite{schmid2025pretrainedsed}, a frame-level sound event detector trained
        on AudioSet-Strong. A sound becomes a picture when its score passes one bar. The bar was chosen on the
        development set only (0.7, the lowest development cost; Figure~\ref{fig:m2dsweep}) and the test set was scored once at that bar.
  \item[Qwen3-Omni, audio] Qwen3-Omni-30B-A3B~\cite{xu2025qwen3omni} hears the clip and lists every sound with its start time.
  \item[Qwen3-Omni, video and audio] the same model watches and hears the clip and lists only the sounds whose source it
        cannot see, with start and end.
\end{description}
The score checks only which sound is shown and when, so these rows count every picture as if it were drawn perfectly.
Table~\ref{tab:onemodel} gives the results; its $p$-values are uncorrected and are not part of the five tests of
Section~\ref{sec:support}.

\subsection{What the statistical tests support}\label{sec:support}
\begin{description}
  \item[Significant.] On the test set the final system costs less than direct audio-to-image generation and audio
        captioning ($p < 0.001$; $0.001$ after the Holm correction below). On the development set it costs less than
        showing nothing ($p < 0.001$ before and after the correction).
  \item[At the margin.] On the test set, the final system costs less than showing nothing ($p = 0.018$ uncorrected;
        0.055 after the Holm correction below) and has a higher F1 than direct audio-to-image generation and audio
        captioning ($p = 0.036$ uncorrected; 0.073 after the correction). Both point the same way as on the development
        set, and both uncorrected 95\,\% intervals exclude zero, but neither stays below 0.05 once the five tests are
        taken together.
  \item[Not significant.] On the development set, the difference from direct audio-to-image generation ($p = 0.085$).
  \item[Multiple comparisons.] Five comparisons of the final system with a baseline are reported as findings, all on
        the same clips. If each were read on its own at the 5\,\% level and none had a real difference, the chance
        that at least one of the five falls below 0.05 by luck would be about 23\,\% for independent tests
        ($1 - 0.95^5$). The Holm correction~\cite{holm1979} holds that chance to at most 5\,\% for the five together.
        It was chosen because it is valid whatever the correlation between the tests (they share clips), it is never less powerful than the simpler
        Bonferroni correction, and it is the correction used for the earlier comparison table
        (Appendix~\ref{sec:sept-table}). The family is every comparison of the final system against a baseline of Table~\ref{tab:headline} for which a
        $p$-value is reported: cost against each baseline on each set, and F1 against direct audio-to-image generation
        on the test set. The two comparisons of one baseline against the other (direct audio-to-image generation against showing nothing, Section~\ref{sec:headline}) are reported with uncorrected $p$-values for completeness and are not part of the family. With a Holm correction over the five, the
        development-set difference from showing nothing stays at $p < 0.001$, the test-set cost difference from direct
        audio-to-image generation becomes $p = 0.001$, the test-set difference from showing nothing becomes
        $p = 0.055$, the test-set F1 difference becomes $p = 0.073$, and the development-set difference from direct
        audio-to-image generation stays at $p = 0.085$.
  \item[Reproduced.] Every count was reproduced by two independent routes, the scoring harness and the logged
        decision trail, with no difference on any of the 158 clips (Appendix~\ref{sec:parity}).
\end{description}

\subsection{Sensitivity to the cost of a wrong picture}\label{sec:sensitivity}
The cost of one wrong picture ($\beta$, with a missed sound fixed at 4) is the one number in the metric that only deaf
and hard-of-hearing viewers can set. A viewer study to measure it was designed but not run, so the main analysis
assumes $\beta = 2$. Figure~\ref{fig:beta} shows how the conclusions change if $\beta$ is different.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/beta_sensitivity.pdf}
\caption{Cost per clip as the cost of a wrong picture ($\beta$) varies, with a missed sound fixed at 4 (point estimates).
Dots mark where a system breaks even with showing nothing: the final system at $\beta = 4.5$ (test) and $7.7$
(development, off the chart), direct audio-to-image generation at $\beta = 1.9$ (test) and $3.5$ (development). With
95\,\% bootstrap intervals, the final system is cheaper than showing nothing up to about $\beta = 2.34$ (test) and
$\beta = 4.4$ (development).}
\label{fig:beta}
\end{figure}

On point estimates, the final system is cheaper than showing nothing for every $\beta < 4.5$ on the test set, that
is, until a wrong picture hurts slightly more than a missed sound, and cheaper than direct audio-to-image generation for every
$\beta$ above 0.32 (test) or 0.83 (development). With intervals, recomputed by the same bootstrap at each $\beta$, the
95\,\% interval of the test-set difference from showing nothing excludes zero up to about $\beta = 2.34$; on the
development set it excludes zero up to about $\beta = 4.4$. Drawing every sound is therefore only preferable if wrong
pictures are almost free.

A second check prices each kind of wrong picture separately (Equation~\ref{eq:costw}): one kind is
set to 0 or 4 while the other two stay at 2 and a miss at 4 (Table~\ref{tab:kindprice}). The rows follow this one
rule; none is chosen by hand. On both sets the final system costs less than showing nothing in every row, and on the
test set it costs less than direct audio-to-image generation in every row. As in Figure~\ref{fig:beta}, the
test-set interval against showing nothing no longer excludes zero once an on-screen or other-sound picture costs 4;
the price of an other-sound picture moves it most, because that is the kind the final system shows most often (15 of 22).
On the development set the difference from direct audio-to-image is zero when an on-screen picture is free.

\subsection{Where the errors come from}\label{sec:errors}
Figure~\ref{fig:errors} breaks down the misses of the final system by the step where each was lost, from its logged
decision trail.
Most misses are sounds that were heard as candidates and then removed by a later rule -- on the development set most often because
the listeners did not confirm a weak detection; on the test set listeners, vetoes and timing account for about equal
shares (12, 9 and 10 of 41). Each of these rules was accepted because, overall, it removed more wrong
pictures than real ones; the sounds it removes are the price. Six needed sounds in the development set and seven in the test set were heard by no detector.
(These counts are for the final system, with 58 and 66 needed sounds.)
Most wrong pictures on the test set show another sound (15 of 22; Figure~\ref{fig:wrongkind}). Figure~\ref{fig:bylabel}
shows the needed sounds found per gold label, with each miss coloured by the step where it was lost: on the development
set none of the four needed bird calls is found, two of them lost at the on-screen check, where perched birds are
taken as the source (Section~\ref{sec:presence}). Two large families of the test set are never found, machine gun (0 of 5) and
vehicle horn (0 of 4), for different reasons. Three of the horns are in one clip with a continuous vehicle sound:
the continuation veto reads each repeated honk as a later piece of the traffic sound already going on; the fourth
is lost to timing. Of the machine guns, two are silenced at the on-screen check, one
falls to the mirror veto (a sound heard only by BEATs is dropped when FlexSED hears another family much more strongly at
that moment and the listeners do not put it back; here a helicopter scored higher), one is lost to timing, and one is merged into a
stronger gunshot of the same family. Section~\ref{sec:hard} explains why these errors are hard to remove.
