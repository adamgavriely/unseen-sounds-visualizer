\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Baselines}\label{sec:baselines}
The project proposal's section on baseline systems names the systems the method is compared against. All are run
on exactly the same clips and scored with exactly the same rules:
\begin{description}
  \item[Direct audio-to-image generation] is the final system with the on-screen check switched off: the same
        detection stack, listeners, display rules and picture model, with a picture for every surviving sound. The
        other steps that read the video to decide visibility, chiefly the depict-event check, are switched off with
        it (Appendix~\ref{sec:baselinedetail}). Because everything else is held fixed, the comparison isolates the
        question the proposal asks: what reasoning about what the video already shows adds to the same detections.
        (Published audio-to-image models draw a whole scene from the soundtrack and cannot be scored per sound;
        Section~\ref{sec:related}.)
  \item[Audio captioning] describes the same sounds in text instead of pictures. Under the per-sound score a caption is
        right or wrong at exactly the same moments as the audio-to-image picture, so it has the same score; the
        difference between text and pictures can only be measured with viewers (Section~\ref{sec:limits}).
  \item[Show nothing] no augmentation at all: the situation of a viewer with subtitles only.
\end{description}
A generator conditioned on the video and audio that would ``generate what is not seen'' (for example MiniMax-H3) was
also raised during supervision. It produces whole new video, which the per-sound score cannot evaluate, so it was not
run as a baseline (Section~\ref{sec:limits}).

On the test set the final system finds 24 of 65 needed sounds, and half of its pictures are right. Drawing every heard
sound finds 26, but only 30\,\% of its pictures are right, and on the test set it is not significantly better than
showing nothing ($+0.227$ per clip, $[-0.409, +0.841]$, $p = 0.49$, uncorrected); on the development set it is
($-0.817$, $[-1.578, -0.085]$, $p = 0.032$). The final system is cheaper than both: by 0.773 per clip than direct
audio-to-image generation ($p < 0.001$), which is 24\,\% of that baseline's cost, and by 0.545 than showing nothing
($p = 0.044$), 18\,\% of its cost.

The win over direct audio-to-image generation comes from giving up a few hits to remove many wrong pictures. On the
test set the final system has 2 fewer hits and 38 fewer wrong pictures: $(2\cdot 38 - 4\cdot 2)/88 = 0.773$ per clip,
or 19 wrong pictures removed per hit lost. On the development set it has 5 fewer hits and 24 fewer wrong pictures:
$(2\cdot 24 - 4\cdot 5)/71 = 0.394$, about 5 per hit lost. Of the 38 wrong pictures removed on the test set, 12 are
on-screen pictures; the rest show another sound or nothing. Silencing a sound also removes its late repeat pictures,
and the depict-event check, which is off in the baseline, removes some more. Figure~\ref{fig:breakdown} shows the
two effects on the cost.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/win_breakdown.pdf}
\caption{Where the gain over direct audio-to-image generation comes from. Starting from its cost (left), the steps that
read the video remove wrong pictures (green, saving 2 per picture) and lose a few hits (red, costing 4 per hit). On
the test set they remove 38 wrong pictures and lose 2 hits; on the development set 24 and 5. Dotted line: showing
nothing.}
\label{fig:breakdown}
\end{figure}

On the development set the direction is the same, but the difference from direct audio-to-image is smaller ($-0.394$,
$p = 0.085$). Two things differ between the sets. Direct audio-to-image shows fewer wrong pictures per clip on the
development set (39 over 71 clips, 0.55 per clip, against 62 over 88, 0.70), mostly because it shows fewer other-sound
pictures there (14 against 32). And the check costs more hits there: five fewer than direct audio-to-image on the
development set, but only two fewer on the test set.

F1, which weighs a miss and a wrong picture equally, is also higher for the final system on both sets: 0.57 against
0.52 on the development set ($+0.050$, $[-0.039, +0.128]$, $p = 0.25$) and 0.43 against 0.34 on the test set
($+0.085$, $[+0.032, +0.142]$, $p = 0.002$; same paired clip bootstrap). F1 favours the final system even though it
values a removed wrong picture no more than a lost hit.

\paragraph{Danger sounds.} The sounds that matter most to a deaf viewer are the importance-3 sounds: danger or a key
moment, such as a siren, an alarm, glass or a baby crying (Section~\ref{sec:needed}). The final system finds 10 of the
13 on the test set, the same number as direct audio-to-image, and 14 of the 17 on the development set, one fewer, while
showing far fewer wrong pictures (Figure~\ref{fig:importance}). The misses are mostly ordinary events of importance 2.

\subsection{What the statistical tests support}\label{sec:support}
\begin{description}
  \item[Significant.] On the test set the final system costs less than direct audio-to-image generation and audio
        captioning ($p < 0.001$), has a higher F1 than them ($p = 0.002$), and costs less than showing nothing
        ($p = 0.044$). On the development set it costs less than
        showing nothing ($p < 0.001$).
  \item[Not significant.] On the development set, the difference from direct audio-to-image generation ($p = 0.085$).
  \item[Multiple comparisons.] The family is the five tests that carry the report's claims: the cost of the final
        system against each baseline on each set, and its F1 against direct audio-to-image generation on the test set.
        The two tests of one baseline against the other are descriptive. With a Holm correction over the five, the
        test-set cost difference from direct audio-to-image generation and the development-set difference from showing
        nothing stay at $p < 0.001$, the test-set F1 difference becomes $p = 0.007$, the test-set difference from
        showing nothing becomes $p = 0.087$, and the development-set difference from direct audio-to-image generation
        becomes $p = 0.087$.
  \item[Reproduced.] Every count was reproduced by two independent routes, the scoring harness and the logged
        decision trail, with no difference on any of the 159 clips (Appendix~\ref{sec:parity}).
  \item[Test set.] Every decision was made on the development set. The test set was scored after each accepted
        change but never used to choose (Section~\ref{sec:limits}).
\end{description}

\subsection{Sensitivity to the cost of a wrong picture}\label{sec:sensitivity}
The cost of one wrong picture ($\beta$, with a missed sound fixed at 4) is the one number in the metric that only deaf
and hard-of-hearing viewers can set. A viewer study to measure it was designed but not run, so the main analysis
assumes $\beta = 2$. Figure~\ref{fig:beta} shows how the conclusions change if $\beta$ is different.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/beta_sensitivity.pdf}
\caption{Cost per clip as the cost of a wrong picture ($\beta$) varies, with a missed sound fixed at 4 (point estimates).
Dots mark where a system breaks even with showing nothing: the final system at $\beta = 4.0$ (test) and $7.7$
(development, off the chart), direct audio-to-image generation at $\beta = 1.7$ (test) and $3.5$ (development).}
\label{fig:beta}
\end{figure}

On point estimates, the final system is cheaper than showing nothing for every $\beta < 4.0$ on the test set, that
is, until a wrong picture hurts as much as a missed sound, and cheaper than direct audio-to-image generation for every
$\beta$ above 0.2 (test) or 0.8 (development). With intervals, recomputed by the same bootstrap at each $\beta$, the
95\,\% interval of the test-set difference from showing nothing excludes zero up to about $\beta = 2.05$; on the
development set it excludes zero for every $\beta$ up to 4. Drawing every sound is therefore only preferable if wrong
pictures are almost free. A second check weighs only on-screen pictures less
(Equation~\ref{eq:costw}). With $w = 1$ the final system costs 1.972 (development) and 2.364 (test), against 2.169 and 3.000
for direct audio-to-image; with $w = 0$ it costs 1.887 and 2.318, against 1.887 and 2.818. So if an on-screen picture
is judged harmless, the two systems tie on the development set, and the final system still wins on the test set.

\subsection{Where the errors come from}\label{sec:errors}
Figure~\ref{fig:errors} breaks down the misses and wrong pictures of the final system, from its logged decision trail.
Most misses are sounds that were heard as candidates and then removed by a later rule -- on the development set most often because
the listeners did not confirm a weak detection; on the test set listeners, vetoes and timing account for about equal
shares. Each of these rules was accepted because, overall, it removed more wrong
pictures than real ones; the sounds it removes are the price. Six needed sounds in each set were heard by no detector.
(These counts are for the final system, with 58 and 65 needed sounds; the ceiling analysis of
Section~\ref{sec:ceiling} used an older run with 55.)
Most wrong pictures on the test set show another sound (15 of 24). Section~\ref{sec:hard} explains why these errors are
hard to remove.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/error_breakdown.pdf}
\caption{Misses of the final system by the step where the sound was lost (left), and wrong pictures by kind (right).
Vetoes are rules that drop a candidate because another audio model does not hear it, or because it is a later piece
of a sound already going on (the continuation veto); listeners are the audio-language
models that confirm or add weak sounds; ``timing, length and grouping'' covers pictures that start too early or late,
sounds too short to show, and sounds merged into a neighbour. The mapping from logged step to bar is given in
Appendix~\ref{app:sources}.}
\label{fig:errors}
\end{figure}
