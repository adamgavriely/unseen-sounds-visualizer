\section{The system}\label{sec:system}

This section describes the final system as the released code runs it.
Appendix~\ref{app:config} gives every step and threshold.

\subsection{Overview}
Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} shows the data flow and Figure~\ref{fig:example} an output. For one video (stage numbers as in Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}):
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Hear candidates (4a).} Two sound detectors listen to the whole clip: BEATs (a tagger for the 527 AudioSet
        classes) and FlexSED (a detector that is asked one sound name at a time). Anything either hears strongly enough
        for at least 0.3\,s becomes a candidate with a start and an end.
  \item \textbf{Check candidates (4b).} A candidate heard by only one detector, or heard weakly, is doubtful. Two
        audio-language models (the ``listeners'') and a third detector give second opinions. Two kinds of rule use
        them: a \emph{veto} drops a candidate that another audio model does not hear; a \emph{rescue} keeps a weak
        candidate that a listener names. The models are listed in Table~\ref{tab:stages}.
  \item \textbf{Witness rule (4b).} A remaining weak candidate survives if one of three witnesses backs it: DASM~\cite{cai2025dasm}, a third detector, clearly
        hears it at that time; or both listeners list it; or one listener lists it and the vision model says the sound
        fits the scene.
  \item \textbf{Refine (4c).} The start of each BEATs sound is moved to where its evidence begins (never earlier than
        detected), and rescued sounds pass three last filters: FineLAP and DASM must hear them, and only one rescue
        per family is kept.
  \item \textbf{On-screen check, the gate (5).} A vision-language model (Qwen3.8-27B) looks at frames around the sound.
        If the thing making the sound is visibly making it, no picture is shown.
  \item \textbf{Choose the subject (5b).} A sound is also silenced when a more specific sound of its family is visible
        (kinship). For each remaining sound the vision model chooses the subject phrase, 2--6 words saying what to
        draw.
  \item \textbf{Draw (6).} A short, guarded prompt is written for each remaining sound; Qwen-Image draws it; the vision
        model checks that the picture shows the intended thing and it is redrawn up to five times.
  \item \textbf{Clean the display (6b).} Repeats less than 2.5\,s apart become one picture. A listener decides
        whether two same-family pictures up to 8\,s apart are one continuing sound. A picture is also dropped when the
        vision model says the drawn event itself (``a door slamming'') is visibly happening; this second check asks
        about the event, not its source.
  \item \textbf{Compose (7).} Pictures appear in up to three fixed slots beside the video while their sound plays (at least
        1.5\,s).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Detection}
The two detectors complement each other: BEATs is strong on clear sounds, FlexSED hears quiet sounds under speech and
music far better. Most of the development effort went into deciding which weak candidates to keep. The listeners are
asked about each weak candidate in two ways -- a yes/no question (``Is the sound of a siren present in this
recording?'') and an open list (``List every distinct non-speech sound you hear''). The witness rule rests on how often the listeners agree (Section~\ref{sec:hard}). Speech and music are never drawn; human sounds and all other sound
families with a clear source are (Appendix~\ref{app:config}).

\subsection{The on-screen check}\label{sec:gate}
For every detected sound, the gate cuts its time span into stretches of about 5\,s, shows the vision model six frames
from each stretch and asks three questions (exact prompts in Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}):
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Name it:} ``Name the thing in these frames that is making that sound, or answer: nothing.'' If the
        answer shares a word with the label (``church bell'' for \emph{Bell}) this vote says visible; otherwise a
        text-only follow-up asks whether that thing could make the sound.
  \item \textbf{Choose:} ``(a) you can see it happening on screen, or (b) it is not visibly happening'', asked in both
        orders; if the two answers disagree the vote abstains.
  \item \textbf{Describe:} a one-sentence description of the frames, checked against the sound in the same way.
\end{enumerate}
A stretch counts as visible if most votes say so, and the sound is silenced only if \emph{every} stretch is visible.
This leans toward showing: a source that leaves the frame for one stretch keeps its picture.

\subsection{Pictures and display}\label{sec:pictures}
The picture prompt is built from the detected label, not from free text: the vision model may add at most two
qualifier words to the source's name, word lists remove people and places, and an action is drawn with its maker (a
\emph{bark} becomes a barking dog). A fixed tail asks for one large subject on a plain white background. Qwen-Image-2512
draws it at 1024$\times$1024; the vision model then gets a multiple-choice question about what the picture shows, and
a picture that fails (or contains text) is redrawn, up to five times. In a blind test, the author saw each picture for a
moment and named the sound without being shown the label (one test, by the author only); Qwen-Image pictures were named right 26 of 54 times,
against 14 of 54 for the earlier FLUX.1~\cite{fluxcard} pictures (difference $+0.22$, 95\,\% interval $[+0.07, +0.37]$).

\paragraph{As specific as what was heard, never a guess.} The picture is as specific as the detected sound: when the
detector hears a specific kind, that kind is drawn (a detected \emph{Ambulance (siren)} is drawn as an ambulance, a
detected \emph{Motorcycle} as a motorcycle). What the picture does not do is go \emph{beyond} what was heard by guessing a kind
from the frames. An earlier version let the vision model name the source freely from the frames, and on 50 unlabelled
clips used only to design the pictures it invented things the audio had never established: a whoosh became a person
swinging a sword (twice), a plain siren a police car, a thud a heavy door, and a smash a person smashing a glass. The
reason is structural. Often the sound alone does not tell the kind: a siren sounds the same on an ambulance, a fire
engine and a police car. The frames rarely settle it, because the sources this system draws are by definition off
screen, so the model guesses from the place (a city street suggests a police car); and when the source \emph{is} on
screen, no picture is needed at all. The detectors themselves often report the family rather than the kind: in 13 of
the 53 correct pictures of the final system the detector's label was more general than the human annotator's
(\emph{Alarm} for a siren, a telephone and a fire alarm; \emph{Vehicle} for a motorcycle). A wrongly guessed kind
misleads a viewer more than a general picture of the right family: an ambulance drawn for a police siren sends the
wrong message. The frames may therefore add at most two qualifier words (``farm vehicle'', ``car door''), never a new
noun, and a word list refuses a kind the detector did not report. This version was checked on 30 fresh clips that its
rules were not written from.

\paragraph{Alarm-type sounds.} One family of sounds needs its own step: everything under the AudioSet node
\emph{Alarm} (26 labels, among them siren, fire alarm, smoke detector, alarm clock, doorbell, horns, whistles and
telephones). For these sounds the name of the source is a small signalling device, and the image model draws the main
word large: a siren became a lone red beacon and an alarm became a bell, so a viewer could not tell what was heard. The
general wording already asks for ``the whole thing, never one part'', which works for engines, cameras or gears.
Under \emph{Alarm} it cannot: when the detector hears only \emph{Siren}, the larger things a siren belongs to are
themselves kinds of siren that were not heard (a police car, an ambulance, a fire engine), so naming one of them is
exactly the guess described above. Without a larger thing, the picture collapsed to the bare device. \emph{Alarm} is the one
part of the sound list where these two requirements meet. The picture step therefore asks the vision model (text only) whether the device is part of a larger thing or
a whole object. If it is a part, the picture shows the larger thing in a form general enough to cover all its kinds
(the prompt says ``an emergency vehicle'' rather than a guessed ``ambulance''; a detected \emph{Ambulance (siren)}
is still drawn as an ambulance) with the device going off. The image model still has to draw one vehicle, and draws a
fire engine or a police car with lights and siren; what matters for the viewer is that it reads at a glance as an
emergency vehicle sounding its siren, which the lone beacon did not; if it is a whole object, it is drawn
where a person sees it (a fire alarm on a wall, a smoke detector on a ceiling), with what a person sees when it goes
off (flashing lights, shaking). The same word-list guards as above still apply, and every sound outside the family keeps
its prompt, negative prompt and random seed unchanged. The rule was chosen over a per-sound fix because it is defined by
one node of the ontology, not by the two labels that failed; a general ``is it a part?'' question for every sound was
also considered and rejected, because it would change pictures that were already read correctly. Three independent model reviewers, judging the pictures
blind on development clips, named 9 of 11 earlier pictures correctly (the two misses were the siren and the alarm);
with the rule, all five changed development pictures and 16 further pictures of the family were read correctly or partly
correctly, with none wrong (prompt in Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}). The rule changes only the wording of a picture,
never which sound is shown or when, so the detection and scoring numbers of Section~\ref{sec:results} are unchanged.

On screen, repeats of one sound less than 2.5\,s apart become one appearance, every picture stays at least 1.5\,s and
at most 1\,s after its sound ends, and at most three pictures are shown at once.

The scored numbers come from a scoring program (the ``harness'') that re-runs the pipeline's own decision functions
on saved model outputs; an
independent re-export of every decision reproduces them exactly. The live pipeline differs in four small ways that do
not change when a picture starts (Appendix~\ref{app:config}).
