\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

\subsection{Motivation}
Deaf and hard-of-hearing (DHH) viewers receive speech through subtitles. The rest of the soundtrack is mostly lost.
Captions for the deaf may name a sound in brackets, but the DCMP Captioning Key~\cite{dcmp} tells captioners that a
sound's source ``may be omitted if it can be clearly seen onscreen'', and a study of more than 715{,}000 YouTube videos
found hand-written non-speech captions in only 5--7\,\% of them~\cite{may2024unspoken}. DHH users want to know about
the sounds that carry information -- knocks, alarms, sirens, a baby crying~\cite{jain2019home,soundwatch} -- and want
their type and timing~\cite{may2025choices}.

What a viewer loses therefore depends on whether the source is visible. A siren over a visible ambulance tells the
viewer nothing new. A siren over a crowd looking up \emph{is} the scene. This project targets the second case.

\subsection{Problem}\label{sec:problem}
Given a video, produce the same video with a side panel that shows a picture of a sound \emph{while} the sound is
heard and its source is \emph{not} on screen, and shows nothing otherwise. Speech and music are never drawn (they have
their own channels); human sounds such as laughter, coughing or a scream are. No model may be trained, and the system
must work on one video at a time. For every sound this means four questions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Is there a sound, and when does it start?}
  \item \textbf{What is it?} At a level a picture can show: \emph{dog bark}, not \emph{animal}.
  \item \textbf{Can the viewer already see it?} If the thing making the sound is visibly making it, stay silent.
  \item \textbf{What should the picture show?}
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Research questions}\label{sec:rqs}
From the project proposal (6 August 2026); Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} answers each.
\begin{enumerate}[label=RQ\arabic*.]
  \item Which types of audio information contribute most to scene understanding when presented visually?
  \item What level of semantic granularity should be used to represent ambient sounds?
  \item Can automatically generated visual augmentations improve accessibility beyond subtitles?
  \item How should semantic audio augmentation systems be evaluated?
  \item How does the proposed pipeline compare with representative audio-to-visual approaches?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Contributions}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A task and a metric}: a precise rule for when a picture is needed, and a per-sound score that counts
        found sounds, wrong pictures and their cost to a viewer (Section~\ref{sec:task}).
  \item \textbf{A labelled benchmark}: 159 video clips with every sound timed and marked visible or not
        (Section~\ref{sec:benchmark}).
  \item \textbf{A working system} built only from open models, runnable on one video (Section~\ref{sec:system}).
  \item \textbf{Results and an error analysis} that show where today's open models stop
        (Sections~\ref{sec:results}--\ref{sec:analysis}).
  \item \textbf{A complete, pre-registered development record} of every idea tried (Appendix~\ref{app:history}).
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Results in brief.} On a test set of 88 clips the final system finds 24 of the 65 sounds a deaf viewer would
need, and half of its 48 pictures are right. Its cost to a viewer (a miss costs 4, a wrong picture 2) is 2.409 per
clip, significantly lower than showing nothing (2.955, $p = 0.047$) and lower than the September version of the system
(2.909). The on-screen check is what makes the difference: it removes about half a wrong picture per clip
($p < 0.001$). Most remaining misses are sounds the detectors barely hear under speech or music; most remaining wrong
pictures show another sound, or an object that is visible but is not the one making the sound.

\paragraph{How the report is organised.} Sections~\ref{sec:task}--\ref{sec:system} define the task, the data and
the system; Section~\ref{sec:results} gives the results, Section~\ref{sec:hard} explains why the problem is hard for today's
models and Section~\ref{sec:analysis} analyses the remaining errors;
Section~\ref{sec:limits} lists every threat to validity and the limits. The appendices hold the prompts, the detection
details and configuration, reproduction steps with the source of every number, a glossary of internal names, and the
