\begin{abstract}
Subtitles carry speech, but deaf and hard-of-hearing viewers still miss the other sounds of a video. Captioning
practice describes a sound only when the picture does not already show it. The sounds a deaf viewer loses are
therefore those whose source is \emph{off screen}: a siren behind the camera, a dog barking in the next room. This project builds a system that
finds them and shows a generated picture of each one beside the video while it is heard.

Nothing is trained: the system chains open sound detectors, audio-language ``listeners'', a vision-language model
that checks whether the source is visible, and a text-to-image model. Joining them is not plug and play: each model
fails in a way the next one cannot see. Detectors miss quiet sounds under speech, a lone listener's ``yes'' is right
about one time in three, and the vision model answers ``is the object there?'' instead of ``is it making the sound
now?''. Even with the full detection stack, drawing every detected sound without reading the video shows more than
twice as many wrong pictures as right ones on the test set. The final system is the result of more than 150 full-pipeline variants
over more than 30 open models.

On a test set of 88 clips, scored per sound against human labels, the system finds 24 of 65 needed off-screen sounds,
keeping 10 of the 13 danger sounds, with 24 wrong pictures. Its viewer cost (a miss costs 4, a wrong picture 2) is
2.409 per clip, against 3.182 for direct audio-to-image generation or audio captioning and 2.955 for showing nothing
($p < 0.001$ and $p = 0.044$; 0.087 for the second after a Holm correction over five tests).

Most remaining errors are sounds the detectors barely hear under speech or music, and a vision model that confuses
``the object is in the frame'' with ``the object is making this sound now''. Every decision was made on the
development set and the test set was only scored; no study with deaf viewers was run.
\end{abstract}
