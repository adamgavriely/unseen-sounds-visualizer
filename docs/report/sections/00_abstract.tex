\begin{abstract}
Subtitles carry speech, but deaf and hard-of-hearing viewers still miss the other sounds of a video. Captioning
guidelines allow a caption to leave out a sound whose source is clearly seen on screen. The sounds a deaf viewer loses are
therefore those whose source is \emph{off screen}: a siren behind the camera, a dog barking in the next room. This project builds a system that
finds them and shows a generated picture of each one beside the video while it is heard. It also defines the
decision as a scorable task: a per-sound benchmark of 309 timed sounds, each marked by whether its source is seen
making it, whether it is obvious and whether it is important, with a viewer cost that prices a miss against a wrong picture.

Nothing is trained: the system chains open sound detectors, audio-language ``listeners'', a vision-language model
that checks whether the source is visible, and a text-to-image model. Joining them is not plug and play: each model
fails in a way the next one cannot see. Detectors miss quiet sounds under speech, a sound named by only one listener is
real about one time in three, and the vision model answers ``is the object there?'' instead of ``is it making the sound
now?''. Even with the full detection stack, drawing every detected sound without reading the video shows more than
twice as many wrong pictures as right ones on the test set. The final system is the result of 66 pre-registered
rounds, each change accepted only by a rule written before its run, over more than 150 full-pipeline variants and
more than 30 open models.

On a test set of 87 clips, scored per sound against human labels, the system finds 25 of 66 needed off-screen sounds,
including 10 of the 13 danger sounds, with 22 wrong pictures. Its viewer cost (a miss costs 4, a wrong picture 2) is
2.391 per clip, against 3.103 for direct audio-to-image generation and 3.034 for showing nothing
($p < 0.001$ against direct audio-to-image generation; $p = 0.018$ against showing nothing, 0.055 after a Holm correction
over five tests). The audio-captioning baseline, the same decisions shown as text rather than pictures, scores the same
as direct audio-to-image generation under the per-sound metric.

Most remaining errors are sounds the detectors barely hear under speech or music, and a vision model that confuses
``the object is in the frame'' with ``the object is making this sound now''. Every decision was made on the
development set and the test set was only scored. Every count of the final system is reproduced by two independent routes on all 158
clips, and the code, labels, scoring and logged decisions are released. No study with deaf viewers was run.
\end{abstract}
