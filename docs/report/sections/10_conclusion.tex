\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

A deaf viewer misses the sounds whose source is not on screen, and none of the channels reviewed in
Section~\ref{sec:related} supplies them automatically.
The task looks like a short chain of ready-made models, but every link fails in its own way
(Section~\ref{sec:hard}; the missing capabilities are listed in Section~\ref{sec:missing}). This project built a system that works despite these failures, from open models with no
training, after more than 150 full-pipeline variants (Table~\ref{tab:scale}). What is new in kind is the decision
itself made measurable: a per-sound benchmark that marks each sound as seen making it or not, obvious or not, and
important or not, and a viewer cost that prices a miss against a wrong picture for this decision.

On the test set the final system
finds 37\,\% of the needed sounds, half of its pictures are right, and its cost is lower than all three baselines of the
proposal (audio captioning scores as direct audio-to-image generation under the per-sound metric): clearly against
direct audio-to-image generation ($p < 0.001$) and at the margin against showing nothing ($p = 0.044$ uncorrected, 0.087 after a Holm
correction over five tests, on a test set used only for reporting). Its F1 is also higher than that of direct
audio-to-image generation on the test set ($p = 0.002$; 0.007 after the same correction). The remaining errors sit where today's open models stop: sounds the detectors barely hear under speech and
music, and a vision model that takes ``the object is there'' for ``the object is making the sound''.

\paragraph{Answers to the research questions.}
\begin{description}[style=nextline]
  \item[RQ1 -- which audio information matters most?] Danger sounds and key moments whose source is off screen:
        sirens, alarms, a baby crying, glass. They are about a quarter of the needed sounds (30 of 123), and they are
        the sounds the system serves best: it finds 82\,\% of them on the development set and 77\,\% on the test set,
        against 37\,\% and 27\,\% of ordinary events, and the steps that read the video lose none of them on the test set and one on
        the development set, at a fraction of the baseline's wrong pictures (Figure~\ref{fig:importance}). More broadly, the information that
        matters is discrete, sourced events (a bell, a knock), which the benchmark's importance rule (an event one can
        say in a sentence, or danger) captures; steady place noise (wind, traffic hum) is excluded by design, and it is
        also where detectors, gate and labels disagree most. Whether DHH viewers rank sounds the same way is untested.
  \item[RQ2 -- what granularity?] Decide and score by \emph{family} (two labels match if one lies under the other in
        the AudioSet tree, so \emph{Bark} matches \emph{Dog}, while a vague label such as \emph{Animal} matches nothing),
        and draw the specific source.
        Matching by family was the one rule, both for merging repeated pictures and for scoring, that worked on new
        clips without a tuned threshold. The picture names the maker (a barking dog, not ``bark'') from a hand-written
        table; on seven known cases only, the table was right 7 times and the vision model's own wording 3 times.
  \item[RQ3 -- can generated augmentations improve accessibility beyond subtitles?] Not shown. What is shown is that
        the system adds information no subtitle carries (off-screen sounds) at a modelled cost below showing nothing,
        and that its pictures are recognised at a glance about half the time (26 of 54, Section~\ref{sec:pictures}). A study with viewers is the necessary next
        step.
  \item[RQ4 -- how to evaluate?] Per sound, against human labels, with the error split by cause and a cost that prices
        a miss against a wrong picture, reported over a range of weights; decisions pre-registered on a development set.
        Automatic protocols with model-written references or model judges failed: they are circular, decide visibility
        at chance, or cannot tell a picture from a text tag.
  \item[RQ5 -- comparison with audio-to-visual approaches?] Against the proposal's direct audio-to-image baseline (the
        final system without the on-screen check, drawing every detected sound), the final system costs 0.773 less per clip on the test set
        ($p < 0.001$): it has two fewer hits but 38 fewer wrong pictures. Audio captioning scores the same as direct
        audio-to-image under the per-sound metric (a separate captioning model was not run; the captioning baseline is
        the same decisions shown as text); whether pictures beat text needs viewers.
\end{description}
