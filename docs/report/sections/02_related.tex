\section{Background and related work}\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Accessibility of non-speech sound.} The DCMP Captioning Key~\cite{dcmp} is the practice the visibility
rule of this project follows: describe a sound only when the picture does not already tell the viewer. May et
al.~\cite{may2024unspoken} measured how rarely this happens on YouTube, and a later survey of 168 DHH viewers with
interviews~\cite{may2025choices} found that they want the type, the timing and the story-relevant sounds, and
want a choice. An EEG study~\cite{revuelta2020eeg} found that standard sound captions lose emotional information.
Beyond Subtitles~\cite{alonzo2022beyond} interviewed 11 DHH viewers about captions and graphics for non-speech
sounds and built an authoring tool on sound event detection; it supports showing \emph{important} sounds only.
Caption Royale~\cite{delacerdapataca2024royale} (39 DHH participants) and prosody captions~\cite{prosodycaptions}
show that visual styling is welcome when it is readable and does not distract. Sound-awareness systems --
head-mounted displays~\cite{jain2015hmd}, home displays~\cite{jain2019home,homesound}, SoundWatch~\cite{soundwatch},
ProtoSound~\cite{protosound} -- show that DHH users want urgent sounds fast and are worried by false alarms; this is
why a wrong picture has a cost in our metric. Earlier work found icons learnable but slower than text at
first~\cite{wiedenbeck1999icons} and peripheral sound visualisations useful~\cite{matthews2006nonspeech}. None of
this work generates imagery for ambient sound in recorded video, selected automatically and gated by what is
already visible.

\paragraph{Sound event detection and tagging.} AudioSet~\cite{gemmeke2017audioset} (about two million 10-s clips,
an ontology of 632 classes of which 527 are released) is the label space of almost every tagger. PANNs~\cite{kong2020panns},
AST~\cite{gong2021ast} and BEATs~\cite{chen2023beats} are clip-level taggers; AudioSet-Strong~\cite{hershey2021strong}
adds labels with 0.1-s timing. Text-queried, open-vocabulary detectors score a free-text name per frame:
CLAP~\cite{elizalde2023clap}, FLAM~\cite{wu2025flam}, FlexSED~\cite{hai2025flexsed} and the audio part of
PE-AV~\cite{vyas2025peav}. Sound event detection is usually scored with event-based precision, recall and F1 with a
time collar~\cite{mesaros2016metrics} or with threshold-free PSDS~\cite{bilen2020psds,ferroni2021dcase}. Our metric
borrows the onset-only collar but makes it asymmetric (a picture may start a little early but not late), and
reports a cost rather than F1, in the spirit of cost curves~\cite{drummond2006costcurves}. A known weakness of
taggers matters here: a quiet sound under speech or music is ranked below \emph{Speech} or \emph{Music}, and most of
the sounds in our benchmark are exactly of this kind.

\paragraph{Audio-language models.} Qwen-Audio and Qwen2-Audio~\cite{chu2023qwenaudio,chu2024qwen2audio},
SALMONN~\cite{tang2024salmonn} and Qwen3-Omni~\cite{xu2025qwen3omni} answer free questions about audio and are
benchmarked on MMAU~\cite{sakshi2024mmau}. In this project two of them (Qwen3-Omni and Audio Flamingo Next) are used
as \emph{listeners}: they are asked whether a candidate sound is present and to list what they hear, and their
answers decide whether a weak detection is kept. Their free text never reaches the picture prompt, because asked
``what is making this sound?'' the listener named a different maker on 35 of 54 sounds (a hen's cluck became ``goats
bleating''; \fp{docs/history/preregistrations/freeze_picture_setup_2026-09-25.md}).

\paragraph{Vision--language models.} Qwen2.5-VL~\cite{bai2025qwen25vl}, LLaVA~\cite{liu2023llava} and their
successors answer open questions about frames and are the only component that can decide whether a sound's source
is visible without a fixed object list. Known failure modes matter for this task: a prior on answer letters in
multiple choice~\cite{zheng2024pride}, weak handling of negation~\cite{alhamoud2025negbench}, and a tendency to
treat a present object as the sounding one, which recent audio-visual benchmarks also report (the project's VLM
panel notes list them, \fp{docs/history/review/vlm_panel_2026-10-01/f2_prompts.md}). Section~\ref{sec:analysis} shows that this last failure is the main limit of
the gate.

\paragraph{Sound-source localisation and off-screen sound.} Self-supervised localisation~\cite{senocak2018localize,
chen2021hardway,senocak2023alignment} and object-aware localisation~\cite{um2025objaware} point to where in the
frame a sound comes from. Juanola et al.~\cite{juanola2025critical} show that many of these models barely change
their output for silence, noise or off-screen audio, which is the case that matters here; training with such
negatives helps~\cite{juanola2025silence}. Off-screen separation~\cite{owens2018multisensory,tzinis2021audioscope,
yoshida2023offscreen} outputs audio, not a decision about what the viewer needs. These models answer ``where?'', not
``whether?'', and none was found that decides ``visible and making the sound now''; two such signals
(an audio-visual localiser and an audio-visual synchrony model) were tested as gate votes and rejected
(Appendix~\ref{app:history}).

\paragraph{Audio-to-image generation.} Sound2Scene~\cite{sungbin2023sound2scene}, AudioToken~\cite{yariv2023audiotoken},
SonicDiffusion~\cite{biner2024sonicdiffusion}, Seeing Sound~\cite{petermann2025seeingsound} and
AudioCanvas~\cite{ge2026audiocanvas} draw an image from audio; TempoTokens~\cite{yariv2024tempotokens} adapts a
text-to-video model to audio. They are the natural comparison family but a poor fit: they condition on the whole
clip (and so draw the scene, not the one unseen source), cannot be told to leave an object out, and never look at the
video. This project therefore uses text-to-image models (SDXL~\cite{podell2023sdxl}, FLUX.1~\cite{bfl2024flux},
finally Qwen-Image~\cite{wu2025qwenimage}) with a short guarded prompt, and isolates the value of the on-screen
decision with a \emph{blind} baseline that uses the same detector and generator but draws every sound.

\paragraph{Evaluation with model judges.} LLM-as-a-judge has documented position, length and self-preference
biases~\cite{zheng2023judge}; audio-caption metrics validated against people (FENSE~\cite{zhou2022audiocapeval},
CLAIR-A~\cite{wu2025claira}) exist for text, not for pictures shown beside a video. The proposal planned an
automatic protocol (a VLM describes the augmented video, an LLM scores it against a reference). Appendix~\ref{app:judge}
reports why it was replaced by per-sound scoring against human labels.
