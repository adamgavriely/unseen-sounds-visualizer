\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Non-speech sound for DHH viewers.} Captioning practice~\cite{dcmp} describes a sound only when the picture
does not already show it; this is the visibility rule used here. Viewers want important sounds, their type and timing,
and a choice~\cite{may2025choices,alonzo2022beyond}; captions lose emotional information~\cite{revuelta2020eeg};
visual styling is welcome when it is readable and calm~\cite{delacerdapataca2024royale,prosodycaptions}. The closest
prior work, Alonzo et al.~\cite{alonzo2022beyond}, studied with DHH viewers how non-speech sounds in user-generated
videos could be captioned and visualised; it asks how to present the sounds, while this report asks which sounds to
show, and when, without a human in the loop. Sound-awareness
tools~\cite{jain2015hmd,homesound,soundwatch,protosound} show that users want urgent sounds fast and are bothered by
false alarms, which is why a wrong picture has a cost in the metric used here, but none of it selects the sounds of a
recorded video by what is already visible.

\paragraph{Sound event detection and audio captioning.} Almost all sound taggers use the AudioSet label
set~\cite{gemmeke2017audioset} (527 classes in a tree, e.g.\ \emph{Bark} under \emph{Dog} under \emph{Animal}).
Clip-level taggers such as PANNs~\cite{kong2020panns}, AST~\cite{gong2021ast} and BEATs~\cite{chen2023beats} say
\emph{what} is heard. CLAP~\cite{elizalde2023clap} scores a sound name against a whole clip; frame-level text-queried
detectors such as FLAM~\cite{wu2025flam}, FlexSED~\cite{hai2025flexsed} and DASM~\cite{cai2025dasm} score it frame by
frame. Detection is usually
scored with event-based F1 and a time tolerance (``collar'')~\cite{mesaros2016metrics,bilen2020psds}; the metric used
here keeps the onset tolerance but reports a cost instead of F1, in the spirit of cost curves~\cite{drummond2006costcurves}.
A known weakness matters here: a quiet sound under speech or music is ranked below \emph{Speech} or \emph{Music}.
Automated audio captioning~\cite{drossos2017aac,kim2019audiocaps,drossos2020clotho} describes a whole clip in a
sentence, without asking whether a source is visible. Dense audio-visual event benchmarks such as
UnAV-100~\cite{geng2023unav100} label events that are both heard and seen, the opposite of the selection needed here.

\paragraph{Audio-language and vision-language models.} Audio-language models such as Qwen2-Audio~\cite{chu2024qwen2audio},
Qwen3-Omni~\cite{xu2025qwen3omni} and Audio Flamingo Next~\cite{afnextcard} answer questions about audio; they name sounds in
free text but give no calibrated confidence (Section~\ref{sec:missing}). Vision-language models such as LLaVA~\cite{liu2023llava},
Qwen2.5-VL~\cite{bai2025qwen25vl} (used in early versions) and Qwen3.8~\cite{qwen38card} (used in the final system)
answer questions about frames. Among the tools tried here, vision-language models were the only general means of
deciding, for any sound, whether its source is visible. Their known failures matter: a preference for some answer
letters~\cite{zheng2024pride}, weak handling of negation~\cite{alhamoud2025negbench}, and treating a visible object as
the one that is sounding (Section~\ref{sec:analysis}), a failure no benchmark measures although it is the central
question of this task.

\paragraph{On-screen sound, localisation and audio-to-image generation.} Self-supervised audio-visual
models~\cite{owens2018multisensory,tzinis2021audioscope} learn to separate on-screen from off-screen sound; they
separate the audio signal and do not name or depict the off-screen source. Localisation
models~\cite{senocak2023alignment,um2025objaware} find \emph{where} in the frame a sound comes from, but localisation maps
change little when the audio is silent or off-screen, so these models still point at a visible
object~\cite{juanola2025critical}; they do not answer \emph{whether} the source is visible. Synchronisation models such
as Synchformer~\cite{iashin2024synchformer} estimate whether the soundtrack and the frames are in step; they assume the
source is in view and do not say whether it is.
Audio-to-image models~\cite{sungbin2023sound2scene,biner2024sonicdiffusion,ge2026audiocanvas} draw a picture from a whole
soundtrack, so they draw the scene rather than the one unseen source and cannot be told to leave a visible object out.
A text-to-image model (Qwen-Image~\cite{wu2025qwenimage,qwenimage2512card}) with a short, guarded prompt is therefore
used, and the proposal's direct audio-to-image baseline is represented by the final system drawing every detected
sound, without the on-screen check (Section~\ref{sec:baselines}). Model judges~\cite{zheng2023judge} were tried for
evaluation and failed (Appendix~\ref{app:judge}).

\paragraph{The gap.} The nearest neighbours each cover one part of the task: captioning guidelines and DHH studies
say which sounds viewers need~\cite{dcmp,alonzo2022beyond,may2025choices}; sound event detectors and audio captioning
name what is heard; on-screen separation and localisation models relate a sound to the frame; audio-to-image models draw
what is heard. Two things are new in kind: the needed-sound rule (a sound is shown only when it matters and its source
is not seen making it), with a per-sound benchmark and a viewer cost that prices a miss against a wrong picture; no
prior work defines or measures this decision. What is new in combination is the system: existing open detectors,
listeners, a vision-language model and an image model, chained so that each one checks the failure of the one before,
selecting per sound and over time only the sounds whose source is not visible and showing a picture for each.
