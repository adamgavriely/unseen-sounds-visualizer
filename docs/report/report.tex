\begin{abstract}
Subtitles carry speech; the ambient sound of a video -- a siren behind the camera, a dog
barking in the next room, glass breaking off screen -- reaches a deaf viewer only if a captioner
chose to write it down. This project asks whether that channel can be automated: given a video,
detect its ambient sounds, decide for each whether its source is already visible on screen, and
show a picture of the event beside the video only while a sound whose source is not visible is
heard. The system is an inference pipeline over open-source foundation models with no training:
BEATs for detection, a vision--language model for the visibility decision and the depiction, and
a text-to-image model for the picture. It is evaluated on a benchmark of 274 hand-labelled clips
in four scenarios against a blind audio-to-image baseline and an audio-captioning baseline, with
an automatic protocol in which a vision--language model describes the augmented video and a
judge scores it against a reference. Under the human-grounded reference the gated system ties
the blind baseline overall (3.17 vs 3.16 on a 0--4 scale, 100 clips), beats it where no picture
is due ($+0.26$, CI excluding zero) and loses where one is ($-0.24$); a perfect gate would score
3.25. Three findings about the evaluation itself are reported alongside: the reference specified
in the proposal is written by the system's own models and biases the comparison by half a point;
a reference built by independent models cannot decide whether a source is on screen better than
chance; and the rubric's asymmetry -- a withheld picture scores 0, a redundant one about 3 --
structurally penalises abstention, which is the behaviour the gate exists to provide.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% Motivation, problem statement, the accessibility gap (subtitles/SDH and sign language
% carry speech; ambient sound is described inconsistently or not at all).
% Source: project_notes.tex sec:gap.
Deaf and hard-of-hearing viewers receive speech through subtitles and, where available, sign
language. Neither channel is built for the rest of the soundtrack. Subtitles for the deaf and
hard-of-hearing (SDH) may name a sound effect in brackets, but style guides such as the DCMP
Captioning Key~\cite{dcmp} instruct captioners to omit any sound that can be inferred from the
picture, and studies of caption use find non-speech information inconsistently rendered and
often absent~\cite{captionlimits}. Sign-language interpretation is expressive but scarce and not
automatable. Field studies of sound-awareness systems~\cite{homesound,soundwatch} show that
deaf users want to know about exactly the sounds that carry information -- knocks, alarms,
sirens, a baby crying, a dog barking -- and that they read icons and pictures faster than text.

The information a hearing viewer gets from ambient sound is therefore lost in proportion to how
far the sound's source is from the frame. A siren over a visible ambulance is redundant; a
siren over a crowd looking up is the scene. This project targets that second case. It differs
from audio-to-image generation~\cite{sound2scene,sonicdiffusion}, which turns sound into a
picture regardless of the video, and from sound-source localisation~\cite{ssloc,offscreensep},
which finds where in the frame a sound comes from without saying what should be done about it.
The contribution is the combination: detect the ambient sound, decide from the video whether
its source is already visible, and show a picture of the event only when it is not.

\subsection{Problem statement}
Given a video, produce the same video with a side panel that shows, for the duration of each
ambient sound whose source is not visible on screen, a picture of that sound's event -- and shows
nothing otherwise. Speech and music are excluded. No model is trained; the system is an inference
pipeline over open-source foundation models, and human involvement is limited to the benchmark
labels used for evaluation.

\subsection{Research questions}
% Verbatim from the proposal (Aug 6, 2026); each is answered in sec:discussion.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Which types of audio information contribute most to scene understanding when presented visually?
  \item What level of semantic granularity should be used to represent ambient sounds?
  \item Can automatically generated visual augmentations improve accessibility beyond subtitles?
  \item How should semantic audio augmentation systems be evaluated?
  \item How does the proposed pipeline compare with representative audio-to-visual approaches?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Contributions}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Formulation of semantic audio augmentation as an accessibility task, with a precise
        definition of when a picture is due (sound heard, source not visible, not speech or music).
  \item A seven-stage inference pipeline of off-the-shelf models (\S\ref{sec:method}).
  \item A benchmark of 274 human-labelled clips in four scenarios (\S\ref{sec:benchmark}).
  \item An automatic evaluation protocol, and the finding that its reference must be grounded
        in labels: one written by the system's own models is circular, and one written by
        independent models cannot decide what is on screen (\S\ref{sec:eval}).
  \item An experimental comparison with two baselines, with paired statistics and an oracle bound
        (\S\ref{sec:results}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}
% Condense literature_review.tex: AED/tagging (PANNs, BEATs), audio captioning, audio LLMs
% (Qwen2-Audio), VLMs (Qwen2.5-VL, Idefics3), audio-to-image (Sound2Scene etc.) as the
% baseline family, SDH/sign-language handling of non-speech sound, DHH visualisation studies.
\textbf{Audio event detection.} Nearly all modern taggers are trained on AudioSet, a two-million
clip ontology of 527 classes~\cite{audioset}. PANNs~\cite{panns} established strong CNN
features (0.431 mAP); transformer taggers -- AST~\cite{ast} and BEATs~\cite{beats}, the latter
pre-trained with acoustic tokenizers -- lead the DCASE challenges (BEATs 0.486 mAP on the same
labels). BEATs is a clip-level tagger; frame-level detectors give sharper onsets at the cost of
recall, which \S\ref{sec:results} measures.

\textbf{Audio captioning and audio language models.} Captioning systems trained on
AudioCaps~\cite{audiocaps} and Clotho~\cite{clotho} describe a soundscape in a sentence, and
audio-language models such as Qwen2-Audio~\cite{qwenaudio} and SALMONN~\cite{salmonn} answer
questions about it. Text is the natural competitor to a picture and is the second baseline here;
an audio LM is used only inside the evaluation, to write a reference that never saw the system.
CLAP~\cite{clap} embeds audio and text in one space and is used to verify the audio LM's claims.

\textbf{Vision--language models.} Qwen2.5-VL~\cite{qwenvl}, LLaVA~\cite{llava} and
Idefics3 answer open questions about frames. They are the only component that can decide whether
a sound's source is visible without a fixed vocabulary of objects, and \S\ref{sec:method} shows
that how the question is asked -- a name rather than a yes, two orderings rather than one --
decides whether the answer is evidence or agreement.

\textbf{Sound-source localisation.} Cross-modal alignment methods~\cite{ssloc,objaware} point to
where in an image a sound originates, and off-screen sound separation~\cite{offscreensep}
estimates whether a source is in frame at all. They answer the gate's question with a number
rather than a judgement; they were not used here because they are trained on data where the
source usually is in frame, and the benchmark's positive class is exactly the case where it is
not. The DCASE 2025 Task 3 dataset, which labels every event on- or off-screen, is used to check
the VLM gate instead (\S\ref{sec:benchmark}).

\textbf{Audio-to-image and audio-to-video.} Sound2Scene~\cite{sound2scene} maps audio into a
pretrained image generator's latent space; SonicDiffusion~\cite{sonicdiffusion} conditions a
diffusion model on audio; TempoTokens~\cite{tempotokens} adapts a text-to-video model to audio.
All are blind to the existing video and are the family this work is measured against. The
picture itself is drawn by a text-conditioned model (FLUX.1~\cite{flux}; SDXL~\cite{sdxl} in
earlier runs), with the prompt chosen by the reasoning stage rather than from the audio alone.

\textbf{Accessibility studies.} Non-text sound visualisations -- icons, directional indicators,
animated effects -- are learnable and preferred by deaf viewers in games and AR~\cite{protosound},
and author-selected sound visuals are welcomed in media; caption research~\cite{captionvstranscript,
livecaptions,lightsub} documents the cost of divided attention, which is why the panel here
keeps a fixed position and shows nothing when nothing is heard. What none of this work does is
generate imagery for ambient sound in recorded media, selected automatically and gated by what
is already visible.

\subsection{Stage 4: detection and onset timing}
% BEATs replaced PANNs (notes sec:beats: 11/14 detector complaints resolved). 2 s window,
% hop 0.25 s, reflect lead-in pad, stamp offset 0.5 s. Threshold 0.35, set once (plan_robustness).
% Onset: hysteresis (0.5 of threshold) + prefix-silencing occlusion (notes sec:onset).
% CAM on BEATs tokens does not work (deltas exactly 0 / -1.5): recorded as a negative result.
\textbf{Detector.} Stage 4 tags audio with BEATs (iter3+, fine-tuned on AudioSet-2M; 0.486 mAP
against PANNs CNN14's 0.431 on the same 527 labels). BEATs is a clip-level tagger, so a 2\,s window
is scored every 0.25\,s and shaped like frame-wise output; a reflected lead-in pad lets a sound in
the first two seconds be heard, and a window's score is stamped 0.5\,s before its end so that an
onset is never early. The swap from PANNs was made after a review of eight demo clips traced eleven
of fourteen complaints to the detector's labels (a siren as ``truck horn'', a crying baby as
``sheep''); on the same clips every one resolved. Speech and music classes are never shown. The
display bar is 0.35, set once on the development split and not moved since (\S\ref{sec:process}).

\textbf{Onset.} A window cannot say where inside it a sound begins, and under the plain stamp every
onset on three hand-checked clips was late by $+0.25$ to $+2.0$\,s. Two standard pieces on the same
model fix most of it. \emph{Hysteresis}: a sound must reach the bar, then extends back through any
contiguous stretch above half of it (an approaching helicopter scores 0.15--0.42 for 1.5\,s before
crossing 0.35). \emph{Prefix-silencing occlusion} inside the first window that fired: silence its
opening $t$ seconds for 26 values of $t$, score them in one batched forward pass, and read the onset
as the first cut that removes a tenth of the class's evidence in logit space. It is class-conditional,
so a voice in the same window does not pull it. Three alternatives were measured and rejected: finer
windows and frame-level detectors (PANNs 10\,ms, BEATs-strong 40\,ms) miss about half the sounds; a
class-activation map over BEATs' 160\,ms tokens moves every onset by exactly $0$ or $-1.5$\,s,
because the model was fine-tuned only through the token mean and no token was ever asked to be local.
Results in \S\ref{sec:results}.

\subsection{Stage 5: is the source on screen?}
% Majority of three signals (open naming, world-knowledge check, open description), every
% two-way question asked in both orderings because the 7B model has a position bias; judged
% per 5 s stretch; one continuous picture per continuous sound (notes sec:vlmvisibility,
% sec:actionvisible). Speech as gate context, never as picture material (sec:speechgate).
The proposal's premise is that a picture is due only when the source of a sound is \emph{not} on
screen, so this question carries the system. It was first answered by a hand-written dictionary of
about thirty labels mapped to CLIP prompts; a sound outside the dictionary could never be marked
visible, so laughter, footsteps and a telephone were structurally ungated. It is now asked at run
time about the sound that was actually detected, from frames sampled across that sound's own span.

\textbf{Name it, do not confirm it.} The VLM (Qwen2.5-VL-7B) is asked to name the thing in the
frames making that sound, or ``nothing''. A yes/no question to a VLM collects agreement rather than
evidence; a name can be checked. What is in the frame is perceptual and is asked of the pixels;
whether that thing makes this sound is world knowledge and is asked as plain text, so a loosely
related object cannot count as the source without a table of which objects make which sounds.

\textbf{The action, not the object.} A baby in its mother's arms was named as the source of the
crying and silenced; a deaf viewer looking at it learns nothing about the sound. A second question
therefore asks whether the thing can be seen \emph{making the sound now}. Three signals vote
(open naming, the world-knowledge check, an open description of the frames); every two-way question
is asked in both option orderings with agreement required, because the 7B model prefers a position
(it answered ``(b)'' in 22 of 23 calls whichever option (b) was). Visibility is judged per 5\,s
stretch, but one continuous sound gets one continuous picture even if its source enters the frame
midway.

\textbf{Speech as gate context.} For each sound with speech within three seconds, one text-only
forced choice asks whether the people on the soundtrack are reacting to it. A yes puts the sound
first in line when the panel is full and can rescue a sound just under the bar; it never reaches a
picture and never overrides visibility. It is not asked for fewer than four transcribed words, since
Whisper transcribes non-speech as phonetic junk. On the 100-clip run it was asked 77 times and
answered yes twice.

\textbf{The frames may choose between the detector's own candidates.} When two labels share both
boundaries within 0.6\,s and are not ancestor and descendant (one sound with two names, from the same
windows), the VLM is shown the frames and asked which it is, with ``cannot tell'' keeping both. The
audio proposes, the video picks, and nothing is added that the audio did not hear.

\subsection{Stage 5: one picture per source}
% Ontology dedup (AudioSet ancestor rule; family vs member by interval subtraction; similarity
% merges need time overlap) -- three attempts recorded (notes sec:dedup). Disambiguation among the
% detector's own candidates from frames (sec:disamb).
The detector emits label families (\emph{Laughter, Snicker, Chuckle}) and a clip once produced
three near-identical pictures of people laughing. Three general mechanisms were tried. Label
embeddings fail because single words embed too tightly (\emph{Dog}/\emph{Cat} 0.90 above
\emph{Laughter}/\emph{Snicker} 0.80). Asking the VLM whether two depictions show one thing fails
because it answers the question literally: ``different'' for 90\% of pairs, including an owl on a
branch against an owl's hoot. A similarity threshold fails because it must be re-tuned whenever a
prompt elsewhere changes, which happened three times. What works is the detector's own taxonomy: the
AudioSet ontology (CC BY 4.0), vendored as child$\rightarrow$parent edges, and the rule that one
label must be an \emph{ancestor} of the other -- a shared parent is not enough, since \emph{Animal}
would swallow \emph{Dog} with \emph{Sheep}. The rule is 14/14 on every pair the demos produced,
with no threshold. A family label explains a member only where they overlap in time (interval
subtraction), so an all-clip \emph{Bird} does not swallow a five-second \emph{Owl}. Depiction
similarity survives as a secondary signal at 0.80, and only for pairs that overlap in time.

\subsection{Stage 6: what the picture shows}
% Event-framed depiction (2-5 plain words, no place, no example sentences), forced-choice
% validation asked twice; FLUX over PixArt; panel with dwell, bursts, three rows.
\textbf{Depiction.} For each sound the VLM writes a two-to-five word event phrase (``glass
shattering'', ``audience clapping''), built from the detector's label, the sound's kind as seen in
the frames and a \emph{place} of at most four words asked for separately. A place replaced the
one-sentence scene description after that sentence's objects were glued onto every sound: an
\emph{Air horn} became ``air horn blaring from silver van'' and rendered as a picture of a van. The
transcript never reaches the prompt: when it did, a \emph{Hiccup} became ``Hiccup on the phone, slow
down, how many''. No example phrases are given; the prompt is built from the detector, the audio and
the video only.

\textbf{Validation by choice.} A yes/no check approved ``man on motorcycle drives past silver van''
as a picture of \emph{Laughter}. A comparative check (is the phrase closer to the label than the
scene sentence was?) was right on eight cases and 11/17 on seventeen, because it measured phrase
length. The working check asks the VLM to \emph{choose}: given the phrase and the other sounds
detected in the same clip plus ``none of them'', which sound does it show, asked in both orderings.

\textbf{Rendering and panel.} FLUX.1-schnell renders the phrase at 768\,px on a plain white
background (chosen over PixArt-$\Sigma$ on twelve real prompts). The panel is divided by
simultaneity, not inventory: rows are the largest number of sounds heard at once (at most three),
a picture is held at least 1.5\,s, bursts closer than 0.8\,s are one appearance, and a cell is empty
when its sound is absent. The first design parked every sound's picture beside the video for the
whole clip, dimmed when silent; a panel that always looks the same carries no information, and a
picture on screen while its sound is absent makes a claim a deaf viewer cannot check.

\subsection{Design process}\label{sec:process}
% Knobs set once on the dev split; failure catalogue (18 entries) with the >=3-cases rule;
% demos illustrate only. docs/plan_robustness.md.
Every earlier fix had been made on the clip in front of us, and each knob had been recalibrated
by the next clip. Since 2026-09-13 the rules are: thresholds are set once on the development split;
every failure seen in a demo is logged in a failure catalogue with clip, stage and confidence; a
design change needs three entries of one pattern or a metric move on the development split; demos
illustrate and never tune; and every change is general and re-measured. The catalogue holds 18
entries, seven fixed as general bugs, one decided (the place veto, off after two right and two wrong
in four uses), the rest open at one of three.

\section{Benchmark}\label{sec:benchmark}
% 274 clips (proposal named 300; usable yield 15-20%), four categories: unseen_ambient, mixed,
% seen_ambient, no_ambient. One annotator; blind re-adjudication kappa = 0.60 (notes
% sec:readjudication). First 209 tags predate any model filtering. Main runs use 100 clips,
% 25 per category.
\textbf{Clips and labels.} 274 clips were hand-labelled into four scenarios:
\emph{unseen\_ambient} (an ambient sound whose source is not on screen), \emph{mixed} (some
sources visible, some not), \emph{seen\_ambient} (every ambient sound's source is on screen) and
\emph{no\_ambient} (speech or music only). The central labelling rule is the redundancy test used
by professional captioners (DCMP): not ``is the physical source in frame?'' but ``does the picture
already tell the viewer that this sound is happening?'' -- wind roar over a rider filmed on a
moving motorcycle is \emph{seen}, and so are waves when the sea fills the frame. The main runs use
100 clips, 25 per scenario, so that abstention is scored on as many clips as augmentation.

\textbf{Yield.} The proposal named 300 clips. Four independent sourcing strategies (keyword
scraping, AudioSet temporally-strong segments, curated film scenes, UnAV-100 overlap moments) and a
three-stage automatic filter all landed in the same 15--20\% band of usable positives, and yield
fell as filtering grew. Yet 36\% of clips that contain ambient sound at all are unseen or mixed:
the phenomenon is not rare, the acquisition funnel discards it. The first 209 tags predate any
model-based filtering and are the unbiased sample.

\textbf{Label stability.} Sixty tagged clips were replayed shuffled with the label hidden: self-agreement
78\%, Cohen's $\kappa = 0.60$. The instability is in the positive class (\emph{unseen\_ambient}
reproduced 12/20 against \emph{seen\_ambient} 19/20), which is the class the gate is judged on.

\textbf{Tuning on the test set, checked.} The labelled clips were split 50/50 stratified by tag,
every threshold swept on one half and frozen on the other, for five seeds: the held-out half scored
higher than the sweep half in four of five, and the sweep chose the same corner of the grid each
time. There is no optimism to remove, because the thresholds are not doing the work; the gate is
limited by what a vision--language model can answer, not by its configuration.

The check is timid in one direction: it almost never silences a sound whose source is off screen,

part of the on-screen shortfall is the check answering a stricter question than the label.

\section{Evaluation protocol}\label{sec:eval}
% Proposal 6.1: VLM describes the augmented scene -> LLM writes a reference -> independent judge
% scores 0-4. Abstention scoring (notes sec:abstain); reference must be able to say "nothing is
% missing" (sec:reference).
\subsection{Three references}
\begin{description}
  \item[model-derived (\tagref{v2}, \tagref{v3})] as specified in the proposal: written by the
        system's own VLM from the detector's own events. Found circular on 2026-09-14 (review): it
        rewards whichever system repeats the detector.
  \item[human-grounded (\tagref{v2\_grounded}, \tagref{v3\_grounded})] the same reference corrected
        by the annotator's label; only computable on labelled clips; raises the abstaining system
        more than the baselines by arithmetic (notes \S{}Main Result).
  \item[independent (\tagref{v2\_indep}, \tagref{v3\_indep})] built by models that never see the
        system's output: Qwen2-Audio listens to the Demucs no-vocals stem and to the raw mix in
        10 s chunks (the union, speech tags dropped; the stem alone was empty on a third of the
        clips because Demucs pulls tonal sounds into the vocals stem); each claim is verified by
        CLAP, kept if this clip's audio ranks in the top 20 of the 100 benchmark clips for the
        claim's text with positive cosine (a mean $+2\sigma$ bar over text decoys, the first form,
        left 58 of 100 references empty against 25 clips tagged \emph{no\_ambient}; both are
        reported); Idefics3-8B looks at the frames for what is on screen; Phi-3.5-mini writes the
        reference (Llama-3.1-8B is gated and was not accessible to the account); a MiniLM sentinel
        ($\tau = 0.50$, uncalibrated) rejects sentences unsupported by the verified list. It was
        built to be the corrected primary; \S\ref{sec:results} shows it cannot score the gate
        decision, so the human-grounded reference remains the one that does.
\end{description}

\subsection{Baselines}
Blind audio-to-image (depict everything heard, no gate) and audio captioning (text instead of a
picture); video reasoning off for both.

\subsection{Statistics}
Per-clip paired win/tie/loss, bootstrap 95\% CI on the mean difference (5000 resamples), and an
oracle-gate bound: the human tag as the gate, the best system's output where a picture is due
(\texttt{scripts/paired\_stats.py}).

On \tagref{v2} the blind baseline scores higher than the gated system under every reference,
with the interval excluding zero each time; the circular reference was not what held the gate
back. On \tagref{v3} -- the shipping configuration: BEATs, per-sound visibility, event
depictions, ontology deduplication -- the gap closes to a tie under the human-grounded reference
($+0.01$, 21 wins to 15 losses) while remaining $-0.40$ under the model-derived one. The oracle
row is the ceiling any gate could reach on this benchmark with the best system's output where a
picture is due: \tagref{v3} sits 0.08 below it. The gated system also beats the captioning
baseline on \tagref{v3\_grounded} ($+0.28$, $[+0.10, +0.46]$).

\textbf{The independent reference is not neutral either.} Its silence decision (``nothing beyond
the picture'') agrees with the human tag on 55 of 100 clips -- chance. The list-and-match
visibility step (Idefics3 asked for a list of sound-capable things, one word back per clip;
MiniLM cosine to each verified sound) calls almost every sound unseen, so on seen-ambient clips
the reference says the viewer would miss the sound and the blind baseline is rewarded for
showing it (seen\_ambient: blind 3.08, proposed 2.48). A per-sound question to the same VLM
(``is the thing producing \emph{X} visible? answer \texttt{visible: <name>} or \texttt{not
visible}'') was tried next and scored the same 55\%, because the rule ``silent only if every
verified claim is visible'' fails whenever one of five to eight audio-LM claims (\emph{video
game sound}, \emph{wind noise (microphone)}, \emph{clock ticking}) is not; that variant was
not judged (a 70\% agreement bar gates the judge pass). The reference's remaining weakness is
therefore the same question the system itself finds hardest -- whether a sound's source is on
screen -- and the honest reading is that no automatic reference built here can score the gate
decision; the human-grounded reference remains the only one that can, with the asymmetry
already stated.

\subsection{Cost sensitivity of the gate decision (secondary, post-hoc)}\label{sec:cost}
% scripts/cost_sensitivity.py v3_grounded -> benchmark/cost_sensitivity_v3_grounded.json;
% added after unblinding, at Adam's question "showing what you can see is bad, but how bad?"
% Three-reviewer panel, 2026-09-15: secondary, ratio swept not chosen, headline unchanged.
The pre-registered protocol prices the two errors asymmetrically: a withheld picture scores 0
where one would have scored up to 4, a redundant picture about 3 where silence scores 4 -- a
redundant picture costs roughly a quarter of a withheld one. That ratio is a property of the
rubric, not of deaf viewers; captioning practice omits sounds whose source is visible because
redundancy has a real cost (\S\ref{sec:related}). We therefore add, as a secondary analysis
declared after the main result, a judge-free score of the clip-level decision with the ratio
as an explicit parameter. Per clip and system, \emph{showed} means the system produced at least
one augmentation and \emph{due} means the human tag is unseen or mixed;
\[
S(r) = 1 - \frac{\text{missed} + r\cdot\text{redundant}}{N},
\]
with $r$ the cost of a redundant picture relative to a withheld one, swept from 0.1 to 1.

\begin{center}\begin{tabular}{lcccccccc}
\toprule
system & showed & hit & missed & redundant & precision & recall & $S(0.25)$ & $S(1)$ \\
\midrule
gated (ours) & 67 & 39 & 11 & 28 & 0.58 & 0.78 & 0.820 & \textbf{0.610} \\
blind audio-to-image & 82 & 43 & 7 & 39 & 0.52 & 0.86 & \textbf{0.833} & 0.540 \\
audio caption & 82 & 43 & 7 & 39 & 0.52 & 0.86 & 0.833 & 0.540 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}\end{center}

\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=0.72\linewidth]{fig_cost_sensitivity_v3_grounded.pdf}
\end{center}

At the judge's implied ratio ($r \approx 0.25$) the two systems tie ($-0.012$, 95\% CI
$[-0.057, +0.025]$); the gated system is preferred from $r = 0.40$ upward and leads by $+0.070$
at $r = 1$ (CI $[0.000, +0.150]$). The claim this licenses is conditional: \emph{the gate is
preferred whenever a redundant picture costs more than about forty percent of a withheld one};
whether real viewers' costs exceed that is an empirical question this thesis does not answer.
The panel's accessibility reading -- redundancy erodes trust in the channel and compounds over a
session, so its cost is likely above the rubric's quarter but below parity, and higher for
alarm-like sounds than for ambience -- is an argument, not a measurement.

This metric scores a clip as correct if the system showed at least one picture when the clip
contains an off-screen sound, or none when it does not. It does not assess whether the picture
was shown at the right moment, for the right sound, or with the right content; the
pre-registered judge score covers those. It also exposes a blind spot of the judge: on the seven
clips where the detector heard nothing on a clip tagged unseen or mixed, the grounded reference
itself reads ``nothing beyond the picture'' and every system scores 4, so a detector miss is
invisible to the judge; here it counts as a miss for all three systems alike. The caption
baseline coincides with the blind one at the decision level (same detector, no gate).

\subsection{Onset timing}
% benchmark/eval_dcase_onset.json (job 28942869, 2026-09-14): 255 DCASE gold events with an onset

Against seven hand-labelled onsets on three clips the plain stamp was late by $+0.9$\,s on average
and the shipping method by $+0.3$\,s; that is an anecdote, so the same question was put to DCASE
2025 Task 3, whose events carry a start frame at 100\,ms. From 255 gold events (nine non-speech
classes, onset inside the clip) one BEATs pass was stamped three ways; an event counts as matched
when a detection in the same AudioSet family starts within 1.5\,s of it. Every error below is
measured on the matched events only -- 66 of 255 (26\%) at the display bar -- so the table
describes where a picture is stamped when a sound is detected, not how often it is.

\begin{center}\begin{tabular}{lccccc}
\toprule
stamping & matched & mean signed & MAE & $\leq 0.25$\,s & $\leq 0.5$\,s \\
\midrule

Hysteresis is the gain: on the 50 events both methods match, MAE falls from 0.43 to 0.31\,s, and it
matches sixteen more events, because a sound that reaches the bar late is now traced back to where
it began. Occlusion adds nothing on average: on the 65 events both match it is better on 25 and
worse on 39 with an identical MAE of 0.34\,s (paired bootstrap difference $0.00$, 95\% CI

within a quarter-second; on knocks, where hysteresis alone is within 0.08\,s, it moves the onset
$+0.26$\,s late. The seven-clip number therefore overstated the method, and the honest statement is
that the shipping onset is within half a second for four detected events in five. Detector
recall on these five-second clips is the larger limit: laughter, footsteps and domestic sounds
rarely reach 0.35 on BEATs. Whether to keep occlusion is a
development-split decision left open (\S\ref{sec:process}); it does not alter detection or gating,
only where a picture is stamped.

\subsection{Where the gate's errors come from}\label{sec:v4}

The loss on ``picture needed'' clips licenses one change under the process rules: set the
gate's silence rule once on the development split, scoring each setting with the rubric's own
costs (a withheld picture scores 0 where one would score up to 4, a redundant one about 3 where
silence scores 4: $-4$ and $-1$ per clip). The three visibility votes of every sound at or above
0.25 were cached for the 156 development clips with a video file, and sixteen settings were
scored from the cache: silence by majority or by unanimous vote, display bar 0.25--0.40, the
kinds-of-a-visible-source rule on or off.

The shipping setting is the optimum on cost, and every setting that reduces redundancy adds
misses. Two things follow. First, on the development split the gate is not timid: it misses
one of 23 unseen clips and shows a redundant picture on 85 of 133 seen or no-ambient clips.
Second, the sweep could not observe the failure it was launched to fix -- all 25 mixed clips
are in the test set, none in development -- so no tuned variant was run; the shipping numbers
stand.

\textbf{Reading the errors instead.} The cached votes explain both directions without a GPU.
Of the eleven test clips where the gated system showed nothing and a picture was due, seven
are the detector: nothing reached the bar, because the ambient sound sat under speech or
music (crowd hubbub 0.22 under \emph{Speech} 0.84; rain 0.23 under \emph{Music} 0.81; drums
0.29 under \emph{Music} 0.65) or was a texture at 0.24--0.31. The other four are \emph{Vehicle}
in street scenes, where cars are in frame and every stretch was unanimously judged visible
with a stable name (``white car'', ``taxi on road''): under the annotation's own redundancy
rule the gate's silence is defensible and the tag is the debatable one. None is a timing
mismatch. On the development side, of the hundred redundant pictures the VLM could not
silence because it saw no source, about eighty are confident detector phantoms with no source
anywhere -- a whale at a Christmas market (0.36), roaring cats at a helicopter arrival (0.51),
an electric toothbrush in an alarm clip (0.73), glass at 0.87 in a bombardment -- and the VLM
is right that nothing visible makes them. Only about twenty are real sources the VLM said were
absent (thunder in a storm, an alarm in a car-alarm clip), and those expose a definitional gap
between the annotation's redundancy test (``does the picture already tell the viewer this is
happening?'') and the question the VLM is asked (``name the thing making the sound'').

The bottleneck is therefore the detector on both sides -- recall masked by speech and music,
and confident false positives -- more than the visibility judgment. A second opinion from an
independent audio model is the model-based answer to the phantoms: CLAP scores the same two
seconds against all 527 AudioSet names collapsed to ontology families, and a detection
survives only if its family is among CLAP's top $k$, with $k$ set once on DCASE gold as the
smallest value retaining 95\% of gold recall. The check was calibrated
on the 255 DCASE gold events by ranking each event's family among the 328 ontology families
for the event's own burst; the smallest $k$ reaching 95\% recall had to be at most 30 for the
check to reject anything, both declared before the run. Recall was 10\% at $k=1$, 32\% at
$k=10$ and 50\% at $k=30$, with per-class median ranks from 3 (water tap) and 16 (domestic
sounds) to 53 (door), 70 (laughter) and 261 (footsteps): no usable $k$ exists. Together with
the same model verifying claims at chance as an evaluation reference (\S\ref{sec:eval}), this
is reported as a measured limitation of zero-shot audio--text matching on short ambient events
in polyphonic audio, not as a tuning failure; the shipped system uses BEATs alone.

The remaining candidate, source separation in front of the detector (Demucs; re-detect on the
residual, keep the higher score), was assessed and not run: the residual removes speech only,
so four of the seven masked misses, which sit under music, are untouched, and taking the
higher of two scores can only add detections, so it raises the phantom rate by construction.
It is the first future-work item, with its acceptance bars stated in \S\ref{sec:discussion}.
The finding that stands is that in polyphonic video a clip-level tagger's confidence is not
evidence that a sound is in the scene; the detector, not the visibility judgment, bounds the
headline result, and no threshold inside either recovers it.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
% Answer the five research questions with the numbers above. Be plain where the gate does not win.
\textbf{Which audio information contributes most when shown visually?} Discrete, sourced
events -- sirens, alarms, bells, a baby crying, glass breaking -- are what the judge rewards
and what a picture can carry; continuous textures (wind, traffic, rain) are where the detector,
the visibility check and the reference all disagree most, and where the blind baseline's
redundant picture costs it least. The benchmark's yield says the same thing from the other side:
only 15--20\% of candidate clips contain an ambient sound whose source is off screen, but a
third of clips with any ambient sound do.

\textbf{What granularity?} The detector's own taxonomy. Gating on the AudioSet family and
depicting the specific member -- a fire-engine siren, not a siren -- was the one deduplication
rule that generalised (14/14 with no threshold), after label embeddings, a VLM judgement and a
similarity threshold each failed for a stated reason.

\textbf{Can generated augmentations improve accessibility beyond subtitles?} Not shown here;
the evaluation is a proxy. What is shown is that under a rubric that scores what a viewer would
learn, the gated system ties a baseline that draws everything and beats the text baseline,
that it wins where nothing is missing and loses where something is, and that the loss is
mostly the detector's -- sounds masked by speech or music that never reach the bar -- with the
visibility judgment second (\S\ref{sec:v4}).

\textbf{How should such systems be evaluated?} With a reference the system cannot have written.
The proposal's reference is built from the detector's events by the system's own VLM and
favours the system that repeats the detector by half a point. A reference built by independent
models removes that bias but introduces its own: its on-screen decision is at chance, so it
rewards showing. The only reference that scores the gate is the one corrected by the human
label, and it is not available to a deployed system. The rubric's asymmetry must be stated
whenever a gated system is compared with a blind one.

\textbf{How does the pipeline compare with audio-to-visual generation?} It ties it on the
pooled score and beats it by $+0.26$ where no picture is due, at the cost of $-0.24$ where one
is. A perfect gate would gain 0.09 over the blind baseline on this benchmark. The gate's value
is therefore real but small under this rubric, and it lies entirely in what it does not show.

\section{Limitations}
% Mirror LIMITATIONS.md (repository root); keep the two in step.
Stated here rather than left for a reader to find; \texttt{LIMITATIONS.md} in the repository
keeps the full list in step with the code.

\textbf{One annotator} ($\kappa = 0.60$ against their own earlier labels, unstable on the
positive class). \textbf{One judge decides the headline}; a second reproduces the gated-versus-blind
gap but not the text baseline's rank. \textbf{100 test clips, 25 per scenario}: read the
paired intervals, not the means. \textbf{No human study}: a judge scoring a description of a
picture is a proxy for comprehension. \textbf{The references are not neutral}: the
model-derived one is circular, the independent one decides visibility at chance, the grounded
one is computable only with labels. \textbf{The detector's vocabulary and recall}: AudioSet has
no ``phone alert''; a siren under a film score is heard only at its close-up; on five-second
DCASE clips only 26\% of gold events reach the display bar. \textbf{The visibility check is
timid toward on-screen sources} (35\% on DCASE gold) and unstable to a half-second shift in
frame sampling. \textbf{The onset method was over-claimed} on seven hand-labelled onsets and is
reported on 255. \textbf{The evaluation software produced plausible wrong numbers} repeatedly
-- a charitable score for an empty augmentation, a similarity that returned 0.0 for everything,
an audio model that never received its audio -- each caught by reading logs, each now guarded.

\section{Conclusion and future work}
A deaf viewer of a video misses the sounds whose sources are not on screen, and no existing
channel supplies them automatically. This project built the pipeline that does -- detection,
a visibility decision from the frames, an event depiction, a picture beside the video -- from
off-the-shelf models with no training, a benchmark of 274 labelled clips in the proposal's four
scenarios, and an evaluation protocol whose reference had to be rebuilt twice before it could
be trusted. The shipping system ties a baseline that draws every sound it hears and beats the
text baseline; it wins where nothing is missing and loses where something is, because the
rubric prices a withheld picture at four times a redundant one and the visibility question is
the hardest a vision--language model is asked here.

Next, in order: a detector-only benchmark on the labelled clips (recall of off-screen events
against the phantom rate, independent of gate and judge), and against it a speech-aware
detector -- BEATs on a Demucs residual with the higher of the two scores, accepted only if
DCASE recall on speech-overlapped events rises by ten points with phantoms per class up by no
more than two and no threshold re-tuned -- or a polyphonic detector trained on mixtures; an
open-vocabulary audio model where the 527 classes have no word; a second annotator; and a
study with deaf viewers, without which the accessibility claim rests on a proxy.
