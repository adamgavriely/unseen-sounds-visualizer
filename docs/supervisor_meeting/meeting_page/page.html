The one design decision worth 30 seconds — stage 5. The vision model is told which sound was heard and asked what in the frames is making it — "name it, don't confirm it" — because a model asked "do you see it?" agrees. Three differently worded votes per 5-second stretch; a picture is withheld only if the source is visible in every stretch, so errors lean toward showing: for a deaf viewer a redundant picture costs less than a missed siren.

Baselines: blind — a picture for every sound, never looks at the video; caption — the sound's name as text.

Examples of the four scenarios
  • unseen (picture due): a church bell ringing over a square, the bell out of view; a smoke alarm in a kitchen; a dog barking off-screen while fireworks are lit
  • mixed (picture due): the ambulance clip — siren behind the camera, cars in view; a bridge scene with a helicopter overhead and gunshots
  • seen (no picture due): the police-car clip; wind roar over a rider on a moving motorcycle; waves when the sea fills the frame
  • no ambient (no picture due): an interview; a music video; a film dialogue scene

Labelling rule (DCMP): wind roaring over a rider filmed on a moving motorcycle is seen — the rider already says it. Split: 100 test clips (25 per scenario) scored once; 174 development clips for settings. Outside check: on DCASE's human labels our visibility decision agrees 66.7% ± 6 — it keeps 97% of off-screen sounds and silences 35% of on-screen ones, so it errs toward showing.

One annotator. I labelled alone; re-labelling 60 clips blind a week later I agreed with myself 78% of the time. Nobody else has labelled — that is decision 3.

"Can this be released / is it legal?" Labels and clip IDs yes; the Wikimedia / Internet Archive / UnAV subset yes with attribution; YouTube and film clips no — the same practice as AudioSet.

Deaf and hard-of-hearing viewers: what is known, what is not2 min

The literature justifies the design; nothing yet justifies "helps DHH viewers"

What the literature says

  • DHH users most want alarms, sirens, knocks, a baby crying, a dog barking surfaced — HomeSound (Jain et al., CHI 2020), SoundWatch (ASSETS 2020)
  • Users prefer icons and pictures to text labels — SoundWatch
  • Trust collapses after false alarms; users switch the system off — HomeSound
  • Captioners omit a non-speech sound when the picture already shows it — DCMP guidelines
  • Non-speech sounds are inconsistently captioned or dropped — Kushalnagar et al.; Revuelta et al. 2020

What this project did not test

  • No DHH participants at any stage — the proposal specified automatic evaluation
  • The judge (a text model scoring 0–4) is a proxy for a viewer, not a viewer
  • No timing, trust or attention-load measures; the 1.5-s dwell and 3-slot panel are unvalidated choices
  • A muted-clip check with 2–3 hearing people was designed, not run
  • Whether home/wearable findings transfer to watching video is assumed, not shown

Design choices that follow from the literature: pictures rather than text (SoundWatch users chose icons); alarms and sirens matter most (HomeSound users asked for them first); the visibility gate itself (DCMP: omit what the picture already shows) — with the trade-off that false alarms kill trust, which is why the gate exists at all.

Can say: "on 100 clips the pictures are judged correct against a captioner-rule reference, at 30% less wasted panel time than showing everything." Cannot say: "this helps DHH viewers" — no DHH viewer has seen the output.

"Then who is this for?" "Literature only, so far; whether to say so plainly is decision 4, on the right." Future work, two lines: a within-subject study, 20–30 DHH participants, muted test clips, three conditions (gated / blind / caption): sound-event recall, intrusiveness, trust after a seeded false alarm — preceded by the 3-person hearing pilot to validate the protocol.
