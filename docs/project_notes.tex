\section{Executive Summary}
% ======================================================================
The project builds a system that makes the \emph{non-speech} information in a video's soundtrack
accessible to deaf and hard-of-hearing (DHH) viewers. Subtitles and sign-language interpretation
convey \emph{speech}, but they largely discard ambient and environmental sound --- flowing water,
approaching footsteps, a distant siren, rain, birds, a reacting crowd, machinery --- which carries
real meaning and atmosphere. The system detects this missing audio semantics and renders it as
\textbf{complementary visual augmentations displayed alongside the original video}, supplementing
(not replacing) the video and its captions.

The distinctive idea is \textbf{cross-modal gating}: because the \emph{video} is also analysed,
the system augments only what the audio conveys but the picture does \emph{not} already show.
A siren whose source is already on screen needs no augmentation; a siren from off-screen is
exactly what is worth illustrating. This ``reason about the gap between what is heard and what is
seen'' framing is the core contribution and the axis on which the method is compared to baselines
that ignore the visual stream.

No models are trained. The contribution is (i) the \emph{task formulation}, (ii) an inference
pipeline that orchestrates existing open-source foundation models, (iii) a curated benchmark, and
(iv) an automatic evaluation protocol. Work is scoped to roughly two months on a modest local GPU
(GTX~1060 6\,GB) with a university GPU requested for the diffusion and large-VLM stages.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Status at time of writing.} Project definition refined and locked; literature
review drafted (this document); model/dataset shortlist and hardware plan produced; two-month
schedule produced. Implementation of the new pipeline not yet started --- a prior code skeleton
(FFmpeg audio extraction, Whisper ASR wiring, modular stage architecture) is reusable.

\section{Project Definition and Scope}
% ======================================================================
\subsection{Problem statement}
Given a short video clip (10--20\,s) with synchronised audio and video, identify semantic
information in the audio --- with emphasis on ambient and environmental sound --- that is
\emph{insufficiently conveyed by the visual stream}, and generate complementary visual
augmentations that communicate it to a DHH viewer.

\subsection{Inputs and outputs}
\textbf{Input:} a short clip with synchronised video + audio. Candidate sources: movie/TV scenes,
documentaries, real-world recordings. The audio may contain speech, ambient sound, environmental
sound, discrete acoustic events, and background music.
\textbf{Output:} a set of complementary visual augmentations shown alongside the original video.
Static, storyboard-style images first; short generated video segments are a stretch goal.

\subsection{The seven-stage pipeline}
The proposal defines a modular inference pipeline in which each stage can be evaluated or replaced
independently:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Audio extraction} --- FFmpeg.
  \item \textbf{Video understanding} --- what is already visible (Qwen2.5-VL / LLaVA / InternVL).
  \item \textbf{Speech recognition} --- transcribe speech (Whisper Large V3 / Qwen2-Audio).
  \item \textbf{Audio event detection} --- non-speech sounds (BEATs / PANNs / AudioSet models).
  \item \textbf{Cross-modal semantic analysis} --- fuse scene context, speech and audio events;
        decide which cues matter, are \emph{not} already visible, and how to depict them
        (Qwen3 / Llama~3.1).
  \item \textbf{Visual augmentation generation} --- FLUX.1 / Stable Diffusion XL.
  \item \textbf{Evaluation}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Scope boundaries and the ``no training'' constraint}
Off-the-shelf open-source foundation models are glued together; nothing is trained or fine-tuned.
The intellectual contribution is task formulation, cross-modal orchestration (especially stage~5),
benchmark construction, and evaluation methodology.

\subsection{Note on the change of direction}
An earlier version of this project illustrated the \emph{speech} of news clips for Deaf viewers.
The current proposal is a deliberate pivot: the star is now \emph{non-speech ambient/environmental
sound}, the \emph{video} stream is analysed (previously ignored), audio event detection and
cross-modal gating are new core components, output is overlaid alongside the original video, and a
formal 300-clip benchmark replaces ad-hoc news clips. Reusable from the earlier build: FFmpeg audio
extraction, Whisper ASR wiring, and the modular pluggable-stage architecture.

\section{Literature Review}\label{sec:litreview}
% ======================================================================
This review covers the eight areas named in the proposal. It is deliberately organised around the
\emph{role each area plays in the pipeline} so that model choices in Section~\ref{sec:models}
follow directly from it.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Audio Event Detection and Tagging (Stage 4)}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------
This is the engine that turns the soundtrack into a set of labelled non-speech sound events ---
the raw material for augmentation. The field is anchored by \textbf{AudioSet}~\cite{audioset}, a
2\,M-clip ontology of 527 sound classes that underpins nearly all modern systems. Two families
dominate:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{CNN-based tagging --- PANNs}~\cite{panns}. Large-scale pretrained audio neural
    networks (notably the CNN14 backbone) established strong general-purpose audio features and a
    then-state-of-the-art mAP of 0.439 on AudioSet tagging. PANNs remain a robust, lightweight,
    easy-to-run baseline and are ideal for a first implementation on a 6\,GB GPU.
  \item \textbf{Transformer-based tagging --- AST and BEATs}. The Audio Spectrogram Transformer
    (AST)~\cite{ast} applied a pure-transformer patch model to spectrograms. \textbf{BEATs}~\cite{beats}
    (audio pre-training with acoustic tokenizers) is the current practical state of the art and is
    used as the baseline encoder in the DCASE 2023/2024 Sound Event Detection challenges. For frame-
    level \emph{detection} (with time boundaries, not just clip-level tags), CRNN+BEATs and
    frame-level transformer variants (e.g.\ ATST-Frame) lead recent DCASE results.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Relevance / choice.} We need both \emph{what} sound and \emph{when} (time boundaries) so
augmentations can be time-aligned. Recommendation: start with PANNs (CNN14) for fast, dependency-light
clip tagging; move to a BEATs-based tagger for quality; use a CRNN/frame-level model only if precise
onset/offset timing proves necessary. AudioSet's 527-class ontology also gives us a ready-made
vocabulary for the ``which sounds matter'' analysis in Stage~5.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Audio Captioning (Stage 4/5 alternative representation)}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------
Where tagging outputs labels, \emph{automated audio captioning} (AAC) outputs a free-text sentence
describing the soundscape (e.g.\ ``a train approaches as birds chirp in the background''). This is a
richer, more compositional representation and a natural bridge to an image-generation prompt. The
canonical datasets are \textbf{AudioCaps}~\cite{audiocaps} (AudioSet-derived, $\sim$46\,k clips) and
\textbf{Clotho}~\cite{clotho}. Modern AAC systems pair a strong audio encoder with a language
decoder: \textbf{EnCLAP}~\cite{enclap} (neural-codec + CLAP + LLM) and \textbf{SLAM-AAC}~\cite{slamaac}
(current SOTA on Clotho/AudioCaps, using paraphrase augmentation and CLAP-based re-ranking) are
representative. \textbf{CLAP}~\cite{clap} (contrastive language--audio pretraining) is the workhorse
embedding underneath much of this and is directly useful to us as an audio$\leftrightarrow$text
relevance scorer in evaluation.
\textbf{Relevance / choice.} Captioning is attractive as the ``description'' that feeds image
generation, and it is a natural \textbf{baseline} (``audio captioning only, no visual output'').
It also provides the \emph{audio captioning} baseline the proposal requires.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Audio Understanding / Large Audio-Language Models (Stages 3--5)}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------
Large Audio-Language Models (LALMs) fold ASR, audio-event reasoning, and captioning into a single
instructable model: an audio encoder + a modality connector + an LLM. Key systems: \textbf{Qwen2-Audio}
\cite{qwenaudio} (strong general audio understanding; can be prompted for both transcription and
sound description), \textbf{SALMONN}~\cite{salmonn} (speech + audio-event + music encoders on a
frozen LLM), and \textbf{Audio Flamingo~3}~\cite{audioflamingo3} (fully open, long-context,
multi-audio dialogue). Evaluation of these models is itself an active area; \textbf{MMAU}~\cite{mmau}
is a multi-task audio understanding/reasoning benchmark worth citing for how the community measures
these capabilities.
\textbf{Relevance / choice.} A LALM is an appealing way to \emph{collapse Stages 3--4} into one
component and to reason about audio directly in language. Trade-off: a single 7B+ LALM is heavier
and less individually inspectable than PANNs + Whisper as separate, swappable modules. Plan: keep
the modular ASR+AED path as the dependable default; treat a LALM (Qwen2-Audio) as an alternative
Stage-3/4 backend and a possible ablation (``LALM vs.\ specialised modules'').

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Vision-Language and Multimodal LLMs (Stages 2 and 5)}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------
Stage~2 (what is already visible) and Stage~5 (cross-modal fusion) both lean on vision-language
models. \textbf{Qwen2.5-VL}~\cite{qwenvl} is the leading open option: native dynamic-resolution
vision, explicit video understanding with temporal grounding (it can localise events to video
segments), and 3B/7B/72B sizes --- the 7B fits our hardware budget and the 3B is a fallback for the
local GPU. \textbf{InternVL3}~\cite{internvl} and the \textbf{LLaVA}~\cite{llava} family are the
principal alternatives. These same models double as the \textbf{VLM describer} in evaluation
(Section~\ref{sec:eval}).
\textbf{Relevance / choice.} Qwen2.5-VL-7B for video understanding and as the evaluation describer;
Qwen2.5-VL-3B or a frame-sampled call on the local GPU as fallback. Because the same model family
spans understanding \emph{and} evaluation, we should use \emph{different} model instances/prompts
for generation vs.\ judging to avoid a model grading its own output.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Audio-to-Image Generation (Stage 6 --- and a key baseline)}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------
Two philosophies exist. (1) \textbf{Direct audio-to-image}: \textbf{Sound2Scene}~\cite{sound2scene}
learns to map audio into the latent space of a pretrained image generator and synthesises a scene
directly from sound, trained only on unlabelled video; \textbf{SonicDiffusion}~\cite{sonicdiffusion}
conditions a pretrained diffusion model on audio. These are exactly the proposal's ``direct
audio-to-image'' \textbf{baseline}. (2) \textbf{Text-conditioned generation} driven by a
language description of the sound: \textbf{Stable Diffusion XL}~\cite{sdxl} and
\textbf{FLUX.1}~\cite{flux} are the open state of the art. Our proposed method sits in camp (2) but
with the crucial extra step that the \emph{prompt is chosen by cross-modal reasoning} (Stage~5),
not produced blindly from audio.
\textbf{Relevance / choice.} Use SDXL / FLUX.1 for augmentation generation (text-conditioned on a
Stage-5 prompt); use Sound2Scene as the direct-audio-to-image baseline. This contrast --- reasoned,
gap-aware prompting vs.\ blind audio$\to$image --- is precisely what the evaluation must isolate.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Audio-to-Video Generation (Stage 6 stretch goal)}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------
Extending static augmentations to short motion clips is the proposal's stretch. Directly
audio-conditioned video generation is still maturing; the pragmatic route is \emph{image-to-video}
applied to a generated keyframe (e.g.\ Stable Video Diffusion-style motion), keeping the reasoning
in Stages 2--5 and treating motion as a rendering choice. This keeps the stretch goal cheap and
optional and avoids depending on a fast-moving, heavyweight research area.

\textbf{TempoTokens (Yariv et al.~\cite{tempotokens}) --- highly relevant; co-authored by
I.\ Schwartz.} This is a direct audio$\rightarrow$video method: a \emph{lightweight adapter}
(``AudioMapper'', an MLP) maps \textbf{BEATs} audio embeddings into pseudo-text-tokens that condition
a \emph{frozen} \textbf{ModelScope} text-to-video diffusion model, so the video is aligned to the
audio both globally (semantics) and temporally (per-segment); it can be conditioned on audio, text,
or both. Only the adapter is trained --- the T2V model and audio encoder stay frozen --- which fits
this project's ``glue frozen foundation models'' ethos. Trained/evaluated on Landscape,
AudioSet-Drums and VGGSound; introduces the \textbf{AV-Align} metric (energy-peak alignment between
audio and video). Code + pretrained models released (project ``TempoTokens'').
\textbf{How it helps us --- three concrete ways:}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{As a baseline (primary use).} It is the audio$\rightarrow$\emph{video} analogue of
    Sound2Scene: it generates footage \emph{from audio alone}, with \emph{no} analysis of the
    existing video and \emph{no} gap-aware gating. That makes it an ideal ``direct audio-to-video''
    baseline to contrast against our cross-modal method (Section~\ref{sec:eval}).
  \item \textbf{As the Stage-6 motion stretch backend.} If we pursue moving augmentations, it is an
    off-the-shelf, frozen-model audio-to-video generator we could plug in.
  \item \textbf{Its AV-Align metric} is directly reusable to score temporal alignment of our
    augmentations with the sound (Section~\ref{sec:eval}).
\end{enumerate}
It shares components with us (BEATs, VGGSound). \textbf{What it does \emph{not} do:} it replaces/
creates footage rather than augmenting an existing video alongside it, and it never reasons about
what is \emph{already visible} --- i.e.\ it does not perform the cross-modal gating that is our core
contribution. So it is a component/baseline, not the whole system. Compute is non-trivial (ModelScope
T2V + adapter, GPU; temporal conditioning capped at 24 frames), so it is university-GPU / stretch
territory. \TODO{decide TempoTokens' role: (a) the direct audio-to-video \emph{baseline} (recommended,
low effort, strong contrast), and/or (b) the motion-generation stretch backend; check its
licence/compute before committing.}

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Accessibility Technologies for DHH Users (motivation \& evaluation grounding)}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------
This literature justifies the whole project and shapes what ``useful'' means. Two threads matter:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Limits of captioning for non-speech sound.} Standard captioning represents only a
    narrow slice of the soundtrack; substantial atmospheric and emotional information is lost, and
    non-speech sounds are inconsistently or poorly captioned~\cite{captionlimits}. Work on
    visualising prosody/emotion in captions~\cite{prosodycaptions} shows the community actively
    augmenting text with visual cues --- directly adjacent to our idea, but for speech rather than
    ambient sound.
  \item \textbf{Sound-awareness systems.} Jain et al.'s \textbf{SoundWatch}~\cite{soundwatch}
    (smartwatch sound classification/alerts) and \textbf{HomeSound}~\cite{homesound} (in-home sound
    visualisation, deployed with DHH households) establish, from field studies, \emph{which} sounds
    DHH users want to be aware of and how visual sound feedback is received. \textbf{ProtoSound}
    \cite{protosound} personalises sound recognition for DHH users. These ground our choice of
    ``which sounds matter'' and provide real user-centred motivation and evaluation framing.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Relevance.} This is the section that separates the project from a generic multimodal demo:
it names the accessibility gap (non-speech sound), cites evidence that it is real and unmet, and
gives a principled basis for the ``which audio cues are worth showing'' decision in Stage~5.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Benchmark Datasets}\label{sec:datasets}
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------
The proposal names three sources for the $\sim$300-clip benchmark:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{VGGSound}~\cite{vggsound} --- $\sim$200\,k 10-second YouTube clips, 309 classes, each
    with a \emph{visible} sound source. Excellent for clean single-event ambient/environmental
    scenarios; its ``sound source is on screen'' property is useful both as positive material and,
    inverted, to construct ``sound source \emph{off} screen'' cases that stress cross-modal gating.
  \item \textbf{UnAV-100}~\cite{unav100} --- $\sim$10.8\,k untrimmed videos, 30\,k+ audio-visual
    events, 100 categories, avg 2.8 (often overlapping) events per video. Ideal for \emph{mixed
    audio scenes} and for cases with multiple concurrent sounds. CC-BY 4.0.
  \item \textbf{MovieNet}~\cite{movienet} --- 1{,}100 movies with rich annotations (42\,k scene
    boundaries, character/place/action/cinematic-style tags). Best source for cinematic
    \emph{acoustic-event} scenes (sirens, glass, explosions, applause) with narrative context.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Selection strategy (decided 2026-08-07 --- hybrid, pilot-first, assisted labeling).} The
benchmark's ground truth is the gating judgement itself: per non-speech sound, \emph{is its source
visible on-screen?} Clips are categorised \texttt{positive\_offscreen} (salient sound, source not
visible $\to$ system should augment), \texttt{negative\_control} (sound present but source visible
$\to$ show nothing), or \texttt{mixed}; speech-only clips are excluded. Ground truth is
\emph{human-labeled} from extracted keyframes, independent of the detector (so PANNs' misses do not
bias it).
Sourcing is hybrid: \textbf{hand-curated CC clips} (Wikimedia Commons: protests, streets, transport,
weather/nature, emergency) for off-screen positives; \textbf{UnAV-100} (CC-BY) for mixed/overlapping
scale; \textbf{VGGSound} (HF mirror) for negative controls, since it is built so the sound source is
\emph{visible}. \textbf{MovieNet is dropped for clips} --- it ships no raw video (registration +
agreement, movies excluded). Composition: pilot $\sim$30 $\to$ $\sim$120 (fallback) $\to$ $\sim$300
(stretch). Tooling + labels live in \texttt{benchmark/} (\texttt{curate.py}, \texttt{manifest.json},
\texttt{ATTRIBUTIONS.md}); clips are gitignored.

\section{The Accessibility Gap: Ambient Sound vs.\ Subtitles vs.\ Sign Language}\label{sec:gap}
% ======================================================================
This section is the motivational core of the project: \emph{what} non-speech audio information DHH
viewers miss, and \emph{how} the two dominant accessibility channels --- subtitles and sign language
--- handle (or fail to handle) it. It is the evidence base that justifies adding a visual channel.

\subsection{What ambient/non-speech sound do DHH users miss and want?}
Field studies of sound-awareness systems give a concrete, user-grounded answer. Jain et al.'s
\textbf{HomeSound}~\cite{homesound} and \textbf{SoundWatch}~\cite{soundwatch} were built around a set
of $\sim$19 everyday sounds ($\sim$31\,h of data) that DHH users said they wanted to know about:
door knocks/doorbells, a microwave or appliance beeping, a baby crying, a dog barking, a cat
meowing, running water, alarms, sirens, a running vehicle, birds chirping. Two observations matter
for this project:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{These are mostly \emph{alerting / functional / safety} sounds} --- things that
    prompt an action or signal danger in the user's real environment. That is a different need from
    \emph{media consumption}, where the missing sounds are often \emph{atmospheric and narrative}
    (rain setting a mood, an off-screen crowd, ominous music, distant thunder). Existing
    sound-awareness tech targets the former; this project targets the latter, comparatively
    under-served niche.
  \item \textbf{The taxonomy still transfers.} The categories --- environmental/ambient, discrete
    acoustic events, human/animal sounds --- line up with the proposal's three benchmark scenarios
    and with the AudioSet ontology used in Stage~4. \TODO{when defining the benchmark's ``which
    sounds matter'' labels, reuse this user-validated taxonomy rather than inventing one.}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{How subtitles / SDH handle non-speech sound}
The relevant caption type is \textbf{SDH} (Subtitles for the Deaf and Hard-of-hearing), the most
complete form, which --- unlike plain subtitles --- is expected to represent non-dialogue audio:
sound effects, music, speaker identity, manner of speaking, and audience reaction. Conventions are
codified in style guides such as the \textbf{DCMP Captioning Key}~\cite{dcmp}: sound effects are set
in square brackets, e.g.\ \texttt{[applause]}, \texttt{[glass shatters]}; music is marked with note
symbols. \emph{Its guidance is strikingly close to this project's core idea:} ``the source of the
sound effect should be included \dots \textbf{unless the source is clearly seen on screen}'' and
``if it can be inferred through the visuals, it is not necessary to caption it.'' In other words,
\textbf{professional captioners already apply cross-modal gating by hand} --- describe the sound only
when it is not already visible. Stage~5 of this project \emph{automates a principle the captioning
industry endorses but performs manually}. This is a strong justification to foreground.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Where SDH falls short (the opportunity):}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Low bandwidth / lost granularity.} A rich soundscape is compressed to a terse text
    label. A University of Sheffield study found that in \emph{Jaws}, captions told viewers there was
    ``famous music'' but \emph{not} that the music signalled the shark approaching --- so DHH viewers
    lost the suspense the score was built to create~\cite{sheffield}.
  \item \textbf{Emotional/atmospheric information is lost.} An EEG study found that attending only to
    standard compliant captions loses substantial emotional information, and that non-verbal-sound
    captions tend to trigger extra \emph{attentional} effort rather than an emotional
    response~\cite{captionlimits}.
  \item \textbf{Divided attention.} Reading captions competes with watching the scene; DHH viewers
    split visual attention between two sources, causing fatigue and missed on-screen content (visual
    gags, who is speaking)~\cite{livecaptions}. \TODO{design risk to validate: our augmentations are
    \emph{also} visual, so they must not \emph{worsen} divided attention --- placement/timing/salience
    of augmentations is a real design question and a candidate evaluation dimension.}
  \item \textbf{No spatial or continuous information.} SDH cannot easily convey \emph{where} a sound
    is (off-screen left? approaching?) or how it evolves --- exactly the information a visual
    augmentation can carry.
  \item \textbf{Inconsistency.} Non-speech captioning quality varies by provider/regulation; recent
    HCI work is actively trying to enrich it (visualizing speech style~\cite{prosodycaptions,
    speechstyles}; adapting non-speech captions generatively --- CapTune~\cite{captune}), which shows
    the community sees the same gap this project addresses.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{How sign language handles non-speech sound}
Sign language can carry \emph{more} than subtitles: skilled interpreters convey music and sound
through classifiers, mimed sound sources (air-guitar, an instrument), sustained holds for long
notes, rhythm, and \textbf{non-manual markers} (facial expression for pitch/intensity), aiming to
transmit the \emph{feeling} of the sound, not just its name --- as seen in theatrical and concert
interpreting. Limits for this project's setting:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Availability.} Interpretation is live, human, and costly; the vast majority of
    recorded/broadcast media has no interpreter, and where present it centres on \emph{speech} (and
    sometimes music), rarely systematic \emph{ambient/environmental} sound.
  \item \textbf{Automation is unsolved.} Automatic sign-language \emph{generation} remains an open
    research problem (noted in the proposal's related work), so it is not a deployable channel for
    ambient sound today.
  \item \textbf{Takeaway.} Sign language shows the \emph{ceiling} --- rich, expressive, affective
    conveyance of sound --- that terse SDH text does not reach. A generated \emph{visual}
    augmentation is a pragmatic, automatable middle path: higher-bandwidth and more atmospheric than
    a bracketed label, without requiring a human interpreter or solved sign-language synthesis.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Synthesis --- why this project}
DHH viewers miss real semantic and atmospheric information in non-speech sound. Subtitles/SDH capture
it only as low-bandwidth text that loses granularity, emotion, spatial and temporal nuance, and adds
reading/attention load; and they already \emph{manually} decide to describe a sound only when it is
not visible --- the very gating this project automates. Sign language conveys sound richly but is
unavailable at scale and not automatable for ambient sound. This positions automatically generated,
gap-aware \emph{visual augmentation} as a complementary channel that is (a) higher-bandwidth than
SDH text, (b) automatable unlike interpretation, and (c) grounded in a principle captioners already
endorse. \TODO{frame the report's introduction around this three-way comparison --- it is the
clearest articulation of the contribution.}

\subsection{Divided attention: design constraints for augmentation placement}\label{sec:placement}
Because our augmentations are \emph{visual}, they compete for the same visual channel as the video
and the captions --- the divided-attention problem raised in Section~\ref{sec:gap} could be made
worse, not better, if we are careless. The HCI literature gives concrete constraints:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{DHH viewers already spend most gaze on text and use peripheral vision for the
    scene.} An eye-tracking study found viewers fixate captions $\sim$84\% of the time and watch the
    video peripherally~\cite{captionvstranscript}; deaf viewers' peripheral vision is more
    attention-sensitive than hearing viewers'. \emph{Implication:} augmentations can live in the
    periphery and still be perceived --- they need not seize central gaze.
  \item \textbf{Bottom-center placement forces costly gaze switching.} Subtitles at the bottom cause
    frequent focus switches and raise cognitive load; work like \textbf{LightSub}~\cite{lightsub}
    explicitly reduces eye movement. \emph{Implication:} pick a stable, predictable augmentation zone
    and avoid making the eye ping-pong between scene, caption, and augmentation.
  \item \textbf{Concurrent visual sources fragment attention.} Presenting many visual materials at
    once fragments perception and raises load~\cite{captionsigncoexist}. \emph{Implication:} this is
    a direct argument \emph{for} aggressive gating --- show \emph{few}, well-chosen augmentations, not
    a busy stream. The core contribution (deciding when to stay silent) is also the main lever for
    keeping cognitive load acceptable.
  \item \textbf{Referential/spatial cues reduce load.} A gaze/pointing cue toward the referent lowers
    processing effort. \emph{Implication:} an augmentation that indicates \emph{where} an off-screen
    sound comes from (e.g.\ a directional cue) may help more than a free-floating picture.
\end{itemize}

\section{Designing Stage 5: Cross-Modal Gating (the core)}\label{sec:stage5}
% ======================================================================
Stage~5 decides, per detected sound, \emph{whether} to augment (is it meaningful \emph{and} not
already visible?) and \emph{how} (what to depict). This is the project's novelty, so its design
deserves explicit treatment.

\subsection{Worked example on real data (validates the premise)}\label{sec:stage5example}
A CC-licensed London NHS-protest clip (Wikimedia) was run through Stage~4. At $\sim$5.5\,s PANNs
detects \emph{Vehicle} (0.55), \emph{Truck}, \emph{honking} and \emph{air horn}; the video frame at
that instant shows only the marching crowd and a storefront --- \textbf{no vehicle is visible}. This
is exactly the target case: an ambient sound whose source is off-screen, detected in audio but absent
from the picture, and therefore lost to a Deaf viewer relying on the visuals. It concretely validates
both the detector and the ``augment what is heard but not seen'' premise --- and shows why Stage~2
(what \emph{is} visible) is needed to make the gating decision rather than augmenting every sound.

\subsection{The decision, decomposed}
For each candidate audio event $e$ (from Stage~4, with time span) the stage answers:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Salience:} is $e$ semantically meaningful for scene understanding (vs.\ incidental
    noise)? Grounded in the user-validated sound taxonomy (Section~\ref{sec:gap}).
  \item \textbf{Visual redundancy (the gate):} is $e$'s source/meaning \emph{already visible} in the
    scene (Stage~2)? If yes $\rightarrow$ stay silent (mirrors the DCMP ``don't caption what's on
    screen'' rule).
  \item \textbf{Depiction:} if augmenting, produce a generation prompt + timing + (optionally) a
    spatial hint for placement (Section~\ref{sec:placement}).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Two signal sources for the gate --- and why to combine them}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{(a) LLM reasoning over structured evidence.} Feed a text LLM the Stage-2 visible-object
    list, the Stage-3 transcript, and the Stage-4 event labels, and ask it to reason about redundancy
    and salience with structured output. Flexible and explainable, but LLMs \emph{hallucinate} in
    multimodal reasoning --- ``more thinking, less seeing'' amplifies it, and models over-attend to
    one modality~\cite{forkmerge, avattn}. \TODO{mitigate: ground the decision in explicit
    structured inputs (labels/lists), force structured JSON output with a required ``reason'' field,
    and keep the LLM from inventing sounds/objects not in its inputs.}
  \item \textbf{(b) Direct on-/off-screen localization.} There is a mature line of work on whether a
    sound's source is visible: sound-source localization via cross-modal
    alignment~\cite{ssloc}, object-aware localization through audio-visual scene
    understanding~\cite{objaware}, and explicit on-screen-vs-off-screen probability estimation /
    off-screen separation~\cite{offscreensep}. This yields a \emph{quantitative} ``is the source
    on-screen?'' signal --- exactly the gate --- without relying on an LLM's judgement.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Recommendation:} use (b) as a grounding signal that \emph{feeds} (a). The localization model
proposes an on/off-screen likelihood per event; the LLM then makes the final salience+depiction
decision conditioned on it. This reduces hallucinated gating and gives the core mechanism a
defensible, measurable basis (and a natural ablation: LLM-only vs.\ LLM+localization).

\subsection{Architecture options}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Modular (recommended default):} Stage 2 (VLM) + Stage 3 (Whisper) + Stage 4
    (PANNs/BEATs) + optional localization $\rightarrow$ Stage 5 (text LLM). Maximally inspectable and
    ablatable; each signal can be evaluated alone.
  \item \textbf{Omni single-model:} a model that ingests audio+video+text jointly (e.g.\ Qwen2.5-Omni /
    Video-SALMONN-class) could collapse Stages 2--5. \TODO{consider as an ablation/upper-bound, but
    verify such a model can be run within our hardware budget and that its decisions stay
    inspectable; do not make it the default.}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Sound granularity requirements (2026-08-20)}\label{sec:granularity}
Decided from a measurement over 16 real benchmark clips (PANNs at our thresholds):
raw salient events fire at $8.2$/clip, but after family consolidation and the display threshold this
collapses to $\mathbf{0.9}$ canonical sources/clip (max 2, never 3+ simultaneous); median event span
$1.6$\,s with 37\% under 1\,s; without consolidation, sub-labels would duplicate massively
(Vehicle-family fired 28 times, Bird 17, Rain 11 across the sample).

\begin{description}
  \item[Taxonomic: the ``depictable source'' level.] Work at the granularity of a nameable,
    depictable real-world source (Dog, Vehicle, Train, Bird, Siren, Rain) --- \emph{not} AudioSet
    leaves (``Yip'', ``Clickety-clack'': undepictable, unreliable) and \emph{not} superclasses
    (``Animal'': too vague to depict or gate). This is the level at which (a) DCMP says to name a
    sound source, (b) CLIP can check visibility, (c) retrieval/generation has a subject. Split a
    family only when depiction \emph{and} the visible concept both differ (Siren $\ne$ Vehicle;
    Thunder $\ne$ Rain). The \texttt{FAMILY} map in \texttt{src/labels.py} implements this and the
    data shows it is load-bearing.
  \item[Temporal: detect fine, display coarse.] Keep detection at PANNs' frame resolution
    (min span 0.2\,s), but never \emph{display} below glanceability: minimum display duration
    $\approx$2\,s (median event is only 1.6\,s; 37\% would otherwise flash). v1 currently merges all
    same-source firings into one span (coarsest option) --- acceptable for gating evaluation.
    \TODO{Adam: decide gap-aware splitting (e.g.\ split a source's span at silent gaps $>$3\,s) if
    the single-span display feels dishonest on longer clips.}
  \item[Simultaneity: cap at 3 slots.] Max observed simultaneous sources is 2; divided-attention
    evidence (Section~\ref{sec:stage5}) says fewer is better. Requirement: panel supports at most 3
    slots; overflow drops the lowest-confidence source.
  \item[Intensity: continuous, no levels.] Confidence $\to$ opacity is already continuous; the
    benchmark ground truth needs only binary salience, not loudness levels.
  \item[Benchmark ground truth at the same level.] Label GT events with the canonical-source
    vocabulary (the \texttt{FAMILY} parents), binary \texttt{source\_visible}, spans at $\sim$1\,s
    precision --- finer timing does not change gating accuracy and would waste labeling effort.
\end{description}

\subsection{Annotation guidelines and labelling dilemmas}\label{sec:annotation}
These questions arose while hand-labelling the benchmark. They are recorded here so the labels
stay internally consistent, so the protocol can be stated in the thesis, and because several are
genuine design decisions rather than bookkeeping.

\begin{description}
  \item[The redundancy test (the central rule).] The question is \emph{not} ``is the sound's
    physical source visible in frame?'' but \textbf{``does the picture already tell the viewer
    that this sound is happening?''} If it does, the sound is redundant and must not be
    augmented. Worked example: a rider filmed on a moving motorcycle with wind roar on the
    soundtrack. No ``wind'' object is visible, yet the visible speed and motion already convey
    it, so it is labelled \textbf{seen}. Likewise waves when the sea fills the frame, visibly
    falling rain, and crowd noise over a visible crowd. This is exactly the DCMP captioning rule
    (``do not caption a sound if it can be inferred from the visuals'', Section~\ref{sec:gap})
    that Stage~5 automates, so the ground truth stays aligned with professional captioning
    practice rather than a naive object-detection notion of ``visible''.
  \item[Microphone artifacts are not events in the world.] Wind noise from airflow over the
    microphone (riding, cycling, a handheld camera in a breeze) is a recording artifact, not an
    environmental sound with a depictable source. AudioSet's \texttt{Wind noise (microphone)} is
    already non-salient; bare \texttt{Wind} on a motion clip is usually the same artifact and
    caused three false augmentations in the first benchmark evaluation.
  \item[Broadband noise is systematically mis-labelled, and it dominates the error profile.]
    \texttt{Wind}, \texttt{Rain}, \texttt{Vehicle} and machinery are all wide-band, low-structure
    textures, and PANNs confuses them with each other and with plain recording noise --- at the
    low operating threshold the detect-everything policy requires, hiss on a mediocre microphone
    is regularly reported as rain or distant traffic. Consequences: (a)~a large share of
    \emph{suggested} unseen/mixed clips are spurious, so the annotator must judge from the clip
    and ignore the hint; (b)~the same errors are the gate's errors, making this the single
    largest accuracy limitation of the v2-(a) pipeline; (c)~it motivates the VLM upgrade and a
    higher evidence bar for the noise-like classes specifically.
  \item[Machinery heard as ``Vehicle''.] Industrial machinery, engines and power tools are
    routinely reported as \texttt{Vehicle}. Because the two are barely separable acoustically at
    this model scale, the fix belongs on the \emph{visibility} side rather than the detection
    side: the Stage-2 concept for \texttt{Vehicle} also covers visible machinery and equipment,
    so a machine on screen correctly suppresses the augmentation instead of producing a phantom
    off-screen car.
  \item[Music is out of scope, including when it is the only ``off-screen'' sound.] Film scores
    and soundtrack instruments are not ambient sound to depict (decision A6). This bit
    repeatedly: PANNs reported \texttt{Saxophone}, \texttt{Music of Asia}, \texttt{Strum} and
    \texttt{Double bass} as salient off-screen sources on scored documentary and film footage,
    producing clips that looked like strong ``heard but not seen'' cases but were pure
    soundtrack. Such clips were removed and the labels folded into the music sets. The real
    boundary is \emph{diegetic vs.\ non-diegetic}, not ``musical'': a busker playing in the scene
    is a genuine source, though still out of scope for v1.
  \item[Faint background sources with no narrative weight.] Birds behind a passing train,
    distant traffic under a conversation. These are genuine non-speech ambient sound and are
    labelled as such; two mechanisms stop them cluttering the output instead of a special label:
    the display threshold (Section~\ref{sec:granularity}) and the three-slot panel cap. The
    ground truth records what is \emph{there}; the system decides what is \emph{worth showing}.
  \item[Trust the clip, not the suggestion.] The tagger shows the current pipeline's suggested
    label, which is wrong often enough to matter. The label must follow what the annotator hears
    and sees. These disagreements are useful in themselves: they localise detector errors, and
    the \texttt{Vehicle}/machinery confusion above is the largest single error cluster.
  \item[``Mixed'' is the selective-gating case, and the scarce one.] A clip is \texttt{mixed}
    when at least one salient source is visible \emph{and} at least one is not (a visible dog
    barking on a street with unseen traffic). It is the category that demonstrates the system
    augments \emph{selectively} rather than augmenting everything --- the core claim against
    blind audio-to-image baselines --- and it is the hardest to source, hence a dedicated pass.
  \item[Three distinct ways to exclude a clip.] \textbf{BAD} = technically defective (no audio,
    no video, inaudible, still image, near-duplicate); a written reason is required and retained
    as a defect record that filters future sourcing. \textbf{Drop} = a fine clip that is simply
    not a case the benchmark needs (another \texttt{seen\_ambient} when that class is full);
    set aside in \texttt{\_dropped/}, not deleted, and excluded from the metric.
    \textbf{I don't know} = deferral, not a final state.
  \item[Clip admissibility, learned from the BAD tags.] Every candidate must have both a video
    and an audible audio stream (mean volume above roughly $-50$\,dB), last at least 15\,s, show
    real motion (no still-image ``ambience'' uploads), be in colour, come from a distinct source
    video (no two clips from one video, no adjacent segments), and not be so dominated by added
    music that the ambient layer is inaudible. These are enforced automatically before a clip
    reaches the tagging queue.
  \item[Never select benchmark clips by the model's own prediction.] A sourcing pass that kept
    only candidates the gate already classified as the wanted category was abandoned: choosing
    the test set with the system under test is circular and inflates the reported accuracy by
    construction. The inclusion criterion is model-independent --- ``contains a detected
    non-speech sound'' --- and the human tag alone determines the category.
\end{description}

\subsection{Evaluation hooks specific to Stage 5}
The gating decision needs its own metric: on a hand-labelled subset, measure \emph{gating accuracy}
--- correctly staying silent when the source is visible, correctly augmenting when it is not
(precision/recall on the ``augment'' decision). This directly measures the contribution and is
independent of downstream image quality.

\subsection{Rendering improvements grounded in the DHH-visualization evidence (2026-08-19)}
Follow-up research on non-text sound visualization for DHH users (details in the literature review,
\S``Beyond subtitles and sign language'') yields concrete, train-nothing upgrades to our
\emph{presentation} layer, ranked by evidence strength and cost:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Two-level rendering: glance layer + detail layer.} Pattern/icon recognition is faster
    than reading, and colour registers in peripheral vision; deaf viewers' peripheral vision is
    unusually acute. Show a small persistent \emph{icon} per active off-screen sound (glanceable),
    and the rich retrieved/generated \emph{image} only for high-salience events. Cheap: we already
    have the label $\to$ icon set is a static asset.
  \item \textbf{Stable slots, no popping.} Eye-tracking work on dynamic (speaker-following) captions
    found moving text \emph{distracts} DHH viewers; static placement is preferred. Keep each sound
    family in a fixed panel slot for the clip's duration; fade in/out, never jump.
  \item \textbf{Loudness $\to$ visual weight.} SoundVizVR encodes loudness/duration; Fortnite encodes
    distance as opacity; deaf users preferred displays with amplitude+position over spectral detail.
    Map per-frame event confidence/RMS to icon size or image opacity --- one compositor change.
  \item \textbf{Direction from stereo panning.} A pointing cue helps DHH viewers link a sound to a
    place. For stereo soundtracks, per-channel RMS difference gives a free left/right estimate ---
    render a small arrow on the augmentation. \TODO{Adam: many benchmark clips may be mono; check
    what fraction of the benchmark has usable stereo before investing.}
  \item \textbf{Customization presets.} The strongest DHH finding is heterogeneity (``Choices? That's
    the dream''): offer \texttt{off / icons-only / full-imagery} modes --- a config flag, and a
    natural ablation axis for the user study.
  \item \textbf{Onset pulse.} Peripheral vision is contrast/motion sensitive: a brief pulse at event
    onset, then static, draws attention without sustained distraction.
\end{enumerate}
Items 2, 3 and 5 are one-file changes to the Stage-6 compositor/config; item 1 needs an icon set
(e.g.\ CC icons per sound family); item 4 is a small analysis utility.

\section{Model Cards --- Executive Summaries}\label{sec:modelcards}
% ======================================================================
A plain-language summary of every model actually wired into the pipeline, so the
choices are understandable at a glance. Each card is added when its stage is
implemented (research/candidate models live in Section~\ref{sec:litreview}).

\subsection{PANNs CNN14 (Sound Event Detection) --- Stage 4 \emph{[implemented]}}
\textbf{What it is.} PANNs (``Pretrained Audio Neural Networks'', Kong et
al.~\cite{panns}) are convolutional networks trained on Google's AudioSet to
recognise 527 kinds of sound. We use the CNN14 ``DecisionLevelMax'' variant,
which produces \emph{frame-level} predictions (a probability for each sound class
at each instant), so we know not just \emph{what} sounds occur but \emph{when}.

\textbf{What it does for us.} It is the detector at the heart of Stage~4: given
the clip's audio it outputs, for every $\sim$10\,ms frame, the probability of each
of the 527 AudioSet classes (speech, crowd, siren, dog, rain, applause, \dots).
We turn those probabilities into (label, start, end, confidence) events and into
the timeline heat-map used to ``detect everything and plot it''.

\textbf{How it works (simply).} The audio is converted to a log-mel spectrogram (a
picture of energy across frequency and time); a CNN reads that picture and scores
each sound class over time. It was pretrained on $\sim$2\,M labelled YouTube clips,
so it generalises to everyday sounds without any training on our part.

\textbf{Why we chose it.} Light and fast (runs a 20\,s clip in $<$1\,s on CPU ---
no GPU needed), permissively usable, backed by the standard AudioSet vocabulary,
and it gives time boundaries. It is the recommended ``first implementation'' in
Section~\ref{sec:models}; \textbf{BEATs} is the planned quality upgrade later.

\textbf{Inputs / outputs.} In: mono audio (resampled to 32\,kHz). Out: a
$[\text{frames}\times 527]$ probability matrix $\rightarrow$ event spans + a plot.

\textbf{Cost / footprint.} $\sim$327\,MB checkpoint (auto-downloaded once to
\texttt{\textasciitilde/panns\_data/}); CPU inference is sub-second for short clips.

\textbf{Limitations to keep in mind.} In speech-dominated clips, background sounds
(crowd, cheering) score \emph{low} probabilities, so a low detection threshold
(currently 0.05) is needed to surface them --- which also lets in more noise. The
527-class ontology is broad but fixed. Frame timing is approximate. These are the
reasons the ``which sounds matter'' decision (Stage~5) is deferred rather than
baked into detection.

\subsection{CLIP (ViT-B/32) --- Stage 2 visibility gate \emph{[implemented, v2-a]}}
\textbf{What it is.} CLIP (Contrastive Language--Image Pre-training) is a model that maps images and
short text descriptions into the \emph{same} vector space, trained so a picture and its caption land
close together. It lets us ask, zero-shot, ``does this image match this phrase?'' without any
task-specific training.

\textbf{What it does for us.} It is the lightweight ``what is already visible?'' check (Stage 2). We
sample a few frames and, for each candidate sound-source phrase (``a photo of a dog'', ``\dots a car
on a road'', ``\dots water/a river''), CLIP scores how well the frame matches it (softmax against
distractor phrases). Sources that score above a threshold are marked \emph{visible}, and the Stage-5
gate then stays silent on those sounds --- so we augment only what is heard but \emph{not} seen.

\textbf{How it works (simply).} Two encoders (one for images, one for text) were trained on
$\sim$400\,M image--caption pairs to agree; at run time we embed the frame and each phrase and compare
by cosine similarity. Sources with no persistent visible object (thunder, explosion, siren) are simply
not in the candidate list, so they are never marked visible.

\textbf{Why we chose it.} Small (ViT-B/32, $\sim$600\,MB), runs on CPU, no training, and it directly
gives a per-source ``is it on screen?'' signal --- exactly the gate. It is the pragmatic stand-in for
a full VLM (Qwen2.5-VL), which is the planned upgrade once the university GPU is available.

\textbf{Inputs / outputs.} In: sampled video frames + candidate source phrases. Out: the set of
visible sources (with per-source scores), consumed by the Stage-5 gate.

\textbf{Cost / footprint.} $\sim$600\,MB checkpoint (auto-downloaded once); a few CPU forward passes
per clip. \textbf{Limitations.} Zero-shot CLIP is approximate --- it can miss a small/partial source
or fire on a look-alike (in testing, a bright lab flame read as ``Fire'' (correct) but also nudged
``Bell''); the threshold needs tuning; it judges whole frames, not precise localization. A full VLM
or a sound-source-localization model (Section~\ref{sec:stage5}) would sharpen this.

\subsection{Is AudioSet's 527-class ontology enough? (coverage assessment)}\label{sec:coverage}
An honest assessment, because it shapes whether PANNs is sufficient or needs an open-vocabulary
companion.

\textbf{What 527 covers well.} It is the de-facto standard audio ontology and covers the common
ambient/environmental sounds this project targets. The specific cases raised in discussion are all
present: \emph{water flowing} $\rightarrow$ Water, Stream, Waterfall, Gurgling, Trickle/dribble,
Drip, Water tap/faucet; \emph{cat} $\rightarrow$ Cat, Meow, Purr, Caterwaul; plus Dog/Bark,
Rain/Raindrop, multiple Siren types, Wind, Walk/footsteps, glass, applause, machinery. So a sound
being ``absent from a clip'' in our demos reflects the clip's content, not a blind spot in PANNs.

\textbf{Three real limitations (not just class count).}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Music over-represented.} A large share of the 527 are music genres/instruments, which
    are mostly irrelevant to ambient accessibility --- so the \emph{effective useful} vocabulary is
    smaller than 527. \TODO{decide whether to filter/ignore the music/instrument classes for this
    task, and quantify exactly how many of the 527 are environmental vs.\ music (pull the AudioSet
    ontology and count).}
  \item \textbf{Granularity is coarse and uneven.} ``Vehicle'', ``Water'', ``Door'' don't capture
    direction (a car \emph{approaching from behind}), intensity (gentle stream vs.\ raging river), or
    implication --- exactly the information most useful to a DHH viewer. Different appliance beeps
    collapse toward ``Beep, bleep''.
  \item \textbf{Closed-set.} PANNs can only emit one of the 527 labels; a genuinely novel or
    culturally-specific sound is mapped to the nearest class with low confidence, never a new label.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{The deeper point.} Even a perfect ontology would not suffice, because \emph{a label is a
low-bandwidth token --- the same limitation as SDH subtitles} (\texttt{[water]}, \texttt{[siren]}).
The semantic richness in this project must come from Stage~5 reasoning + image generation, not from
the detector's label. The detector only needs to reliably say ``water, here, off-screen''.

\textbf{Open-vocabulary alternative (undecided).} If 527 classes prove too restrictive, the fix is an
open-vocabulary detector: \textbf{CLAP}~\cite{clap} (match audio against \emph{any} text description,
zero-shot) or an \textbf{audio-language model} / captioner (Qwen2-Audio~\cite{qwenaudio}) that
describes sounds in free text. This is both a robustness upgrade and a legitimate research
comparison. \TODO{decide whether to add an open-vocabulary path (CLAP or audio-LLM) as a
\emph{comparison} against closed-set PANNs tagging --- potentially a headline experiment:
``closed-set tagging vs.\ open-vocab captioning for accessibility augmentation''.}
\TODO{the current detection threshold (0.05) and min-duration (0.2\,s) are provisional --- tune them
on real ambient clips once the benchmark exists.}

\textbf{Concrete limitation found on real data (2026-08-07).} A 105\,s CC-BY-SA recording of the
Israeli ``Red Color'' (Tzeva Adom) rocket-alert siren + impacts (Sderot) was run through Stage~4.
PANNs reliably detected the \emph{off-screen impacts} --- Explosion (0.34), Burst/pop, Boom,
Artillery fire, Gunshot --- i.e.\ the ``something was hit but you don't see it'' sounds. But it
\textbf{missed the alert siren itself}: the ``Siren'' class peaked at only 0.025 and ``Civil defense
siren'' at 0.006. The regional electronic warning tone does not match AudioSet's siren classes (which
are dominated by emergency-vehicle wails); the alarm tone + Hebrew announcement were read mostly as
Speech. This is a textbook motivation for the open-vocabulary path: CLAP or an audio-LLM could match a
text description (``rising-and-falling electronic air-raid warning tone'') that PANNs' fixed 527
classes cannot. \TODO{critical-alert sounds (air-raid / red-alert sirens, regional alarms) are NOT
reliably covered by PANNs --- plan for CLAP/audio-LLM open-vocab detection or a small dedicated
detector for such safety-critical alerts if they are in scope.}

\section{Results and Findings (2026-08-24)}\label{sec:findings}
Three findings from the first full evaluation. They are reported here in the form an
examiner would check them, because each is more damaging if discovered than if stated.

\subsection{The gate does not yet beat its own ablation}
On 274 hand-labelled clips (73 positive, 201 negative):

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\hline
System & Accuracy & F1 \\
\hline
Cross-modal gate (CLIP backend) & 49.3\% & 48.0\% \\
Always augment --- \emph{no gate at all} & 29.7\% & 45.8\% \\
Always stay silent & \textbf{70.3\%} & 0.0\% \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent Two observations, and the second is the important one. Accuracy is dominated
by the negative class, so a constant ``never augment'' predictor scores 70.3\% and beats
the gate by 21 points; accuracy is therefore the wrong headline metric here. More
seriously, \textbf{``always augment'' is exactly the blind audio-to-image baseline this
project claims to beat}, and the gate improves on it by only $+0.023$ F1. The central
contribution is, at this operating point, not distinguishable from not gating at all.
The error is one-sided: 71 of 103 \texttt{seen\_ambient} clips receive a false
augmentation, i.e.\ the gate declares sources invisible when they are plainly on screen.

\subsection{Object presence is not event visibility (the substantive finding)}
A blind re-adjudication (Section~\ref{sec:readjudication}) recovered, in the annotator's
own words, what the labelling decision actually turns on:

\begin{quote}\small
``bells heard not seen --- only \emph{building} seen but bell is heard''\
``alarm ringing and \emph{is seen} but the `ring' is not indicated''\
``bird \emph{is seen, not singing} but is seen. so this is confusing''
\end{quote}

\noindent The annotator is judging whether the \emph{sound-producing event} is visible.
Every model used in Stage~2 --- CLIP, SigLIP and OWLv2 alike --- answers whether the
\emph{object} is visible. A bell tower is on screen while the bell ringing is not; an
alarm housing is visible while its ringing is not; a bird is visible while not singing.

This single distinction accounts for all three symptoms at once: the annotator's own
inconsistency on the positive class, OWLv2 ``correctly'' locating sources that the
annotator had called off-screen, and the 71 false augmentations above. It also predicts
that better object grounding --- a larger CLIP, a detector, a VLM --- cannot fix the
gate, because the models are answering a different question from the one the task asks.
\textbf{Contrastive and detection-based vision--language models expose object presence,
not event visibility, and the cross-modal gating task requires the latter.}

\subsection{Label stability, and what it licenses}\label{sec:readjudication}
60 already-tagged clips (20 \texttt{drop}, 20 \texttt{seen\_ambient}, 20
\texttt{unseen\_ambient}) were replayed shuffled with the label hidden, the
\texttt{drop} option removed and a written reason required.

\begin{itemize}
  \item Self-agreement 78\%, Cohen's $\kappa = 0.60$ --- moderate. The instability is
    concentrated in the positive class: \texttt{seen\_ambient} was reproduced 19/20,
    \texttt{unseen\_ambient} only 12/20. Part of the gate's measured error is therefore
    label noise on exactly the category the contribution depends on, which is consistent
    with the object/event confusion above rather than with careless annotation.
  \item Of 20 clips originally dropped, only 1 became a positive; 13 became
    \texttt{seen\_ambient} and 6 \texttt{no\_ambient}. The \texttt{drop} bucket is
    therefore genuine negatives, not withheld positives, so the benchmark cannot be
    completed from material already collected.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Tuning on the test set: checked, and it changes nothing}\label{sec:spliteval}
Every threshold in this project was chosen by sweeping it over the same clips the result was then
quoted on. With five display values, five augment values and a few hundred clips there is real room
to fit noise, and it is the first objection an examiner raises. It is now measured rather than
conceded: the labelled clips are split 50/50, stratified by the annotator's tag so neither half is
scenario-skewed, the sweep runs on the training half alone, and the winning thresholds are frozen and
applied to the held-out half (\texttt{benchmark/split\_eval.py}).

\begin{center}\begin{tabular}{lccccc}
\toprule
seed & chosen thresholds & train F1 & test F1 & optimism \\
\midrule
7   & 0.08 / 0.12 & 42.7\% & 46.6\% & $-3.9$ \\
11  & 0.08 / 0.12 & 42.1\% & 47.1\% & $-5.0$ \\
23  & 0.08 / 0.12 & 45.2\% & 44.0\% & $+1.2$ \\
42  & 0.08 / 0.12 & 40.4\% & 48.7\% & $-8.3$ \\
101 & 0.08 / 0.12 & 43.1\% & 46.2\% & $-3.1$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}\end{center}

There is \textbf{no optimism to remove}. In four of five splits the held-out half scores
\emph{higher} than the half the sweep was allowed to look at, and the sweep selects the same corner
of the grid every time. Scoring the held-out half at the thresholds already in \texttt{config.py}
gives results identical to the frozen ones to one decimal place.

The reason is not that the split is generous but that \textbf{the thresholds are not doing any work}.
The sweep's apparent gains were always within noise, which is consistent with everything else
measured about this component: four Stage-2 architectures within two points of each other, an oracle
detector moving accuracy by $-0.2$, and event-level AUROC of 0.647. A component whose output barely
depends on its own decision threshold is not a component held back by tuning, and no amount of
threshold search will rescue it. That is worth stating explicitly, because ``we would have done
better with more tuning'' is otherwise an easy and untestable excuse for the gate's performance.

\subsection{Sourcing yield, and why the benchmark is frozen}
Yield of usable positives did not plateau, it declined as filtering grew more
elaborate: 22.2\% (naive keyword scraping) $\rightarrow$ 19.7\% $\rightarrow$ 15.4\%,
while the unexplained discard rate rose from 0\% to 46\%. Four independent strategies
(Wikimedia/keyword, AudioSet temporally-strong, curated film scenes, UnAV-100 overlap
moments) and a three-stage automatic filter all landed in the same 15--20\% band.
Yet 36\% of clips that contain ambient sound at all are unseen or mixed, so the
phenomenon is not rare --- the acquisition funnel was discarding it.

\section{Main Result: the Stage-7 Comparison (2026-09-09)}\label{sec:mainresult}
The protocol of Section~\ref{sec:eval} was run over 100 benchmark clips, sampled 25 from each of the
four annotation categories, under all three systems: 300 records, rendered with SDXL, described by
Qwen2.5-VL and scored by an independent Mistral-7B judge.

\subsection{Headline}
Scores are 0--4. \emph{silent} is the fraction of clips on which the system produced no augmentation;
\emph{right to be} is the fraction of those on which showing nothing was the correct output.

\begin{center}\begin{tabular}{lcccc}
\toprule
system & mean & $\geq 3$ & silent & right to be \\
\midrule
\multicolumn{5}{l}{\emph{(a) model-derived reference --- as specified in the proposal}}\\
blind audio-to-image & \textbf{2.94} & 84\% & 12\% & 100\% \\
audio captioning     & 2.68 & 57\% & 12\% & 100\% \\
proposed (gated)     & 2.41 & 70\% & 31\% & 39\% \\
\midrule
\multicolumn{5}{l}{\emph{(b) human-grounded reference --- Section~\ref{sec:reference}}}\\
blind audio-to-image & \textbf{3.09} & 91\% & 12\% & 100\% \\
proposed (gated)     & 2.94 & 85\% & 31\% & 71\% \\
audio captioning     & 2.79 & 67\% & 12\% & 100\% \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}\end{center}

\textbf{The gated system does not beat direct audio-to-image generation on either reference.} Under
(a) it is last; grounding the reference in the annotator's labels closes most of the gap
($2.41 \rightarrow 2.94$, paired $\Delta = +0.53$) but does not reverse the ordering. This should be
stated without hedging: measured on what the system actually shows, the cross-modal gate does not
earn its place against a baseline that simply depicts everything it hears.

Grounding raises \texttt{proposed} by $+0.53$ and the two baselines by only $+0.11$ and $+0.15$. That
asymmetry is not a thumb on the scale --- it is the arithmetic consequence of the gated system
abstaining on 31\% of clips against the baselines' 12\%, and abstention is the only behaviour the
correction re-scores. It must nevertheless be disclosed wherever the grounded number is quoted.

\subsection{Where the gate wins, and where it loses}
Broken down by the annotator's categories, under the grounded reference:

\begin{center}\begin{tabular}{lccl}
\toprule
category & proposed & blind a2i & \\
\midrule
\texttt{unseen\_ambient} & 2.64 & \textbf{3.04} & the gate must choose what to show \\
\texttt{mixed}           & 2.28 & \textbf{2.80} & the gate must choose what to show \\
\texttt{seen\_ambient}   & \textbf{3.36} & 3.24 & the gate must stay silent \\

\end{tabular}\end{center}

The split is clean and it is the most useful thing the experiment produced: \textbf{the gate's
suppression is worth having; its selection is not}. On the two categories where the correct output is
nothing, gating beats showing everything. On the two categories where something genuinely is missing
and the system must decide \emph{what}, it loses to a baseline that does no visual reasoning at all.

The failure mode behind the second row is the one already documented in
Section~\ref{sec:stage5example}: on a clip tagged \texttt{mixed} because part of its soundtrack is
off-screen, a police car visible in frame causes Stage 2 to report the siren's source as visible, and
the system shows nothing at all while the blind baseline shows the siren and scores 3. This is
\emph{object presence mistaken for event visibility} (Section~\ref{sec:findings}), now measured on the
system's output rather than on an intermediate label --- which is what the proposal asked for.

\subsection{Ablation: generating the image is worse than retrieving one}\label{sec:genablation}
The proposal names diffusion (SDXL) as the source of the augmentation image. That choice had never
been tested against the obvious cheaper alternative --- retrieving a stock image for the same sound
label --- so the identical 100 clips were run again end to end with retrieval, scored by the same
judge against the same references.

\begin{center}\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
system & SDXL & retrieval & $\Delta$ \\
\midrule
blind audio-to-image & 2.94 & \textbf{3.12} & $+0.18$ \\
proposed (gated)     & 2.41 & \textbf{2.58} & $+0.17$ \\
audio captioning     & 2.68 & 2.68 & $+0.00$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}\end{center}

\textbf{Retrieval beats generation, consistently and on both image systems.} Paired over 300 records
the two runs agree closely overall ($\kappa = 0.839$, 96\% within one point), so this is a small,
stable shift rather than noise: the ranking of the three systems is unchanged, and both systems that
actually use an image improve by the same amount when the image is retrieved instead of generated.

The third row is the control, and it validates the comparison. Audio captioning produces no image at
all, so its score must be untouched by the generator --- and it is, to two decimal places, across 100
independently re-rendered and re-described clips. A difference there would have meant the two runs
differed in something other than the generator.

\textbf{Why this matters beyond a number.} SDXL is the single heaviest component in the pipeline: it
is what forced rendering into its own pass (Section~\ref{sec:mainresult}), it is why the run needs a
24\,GB card, and the render pass with it took roughly half again as long as with retrieval. Dropping
it makes the augmentations \emph{better}, removes the GPU requirement from generation entirely, and
makes the system runnable on ordinary hardware --- which for an accessibility tool intended to be
deployed rather than demonstrated is arguably worth more than the 0.17 points.

The plausible reason is that a retrieved photograph of a siren is an unambiguous photograph of a
siren, whereas SDXL renders a plausible-looking scene whose subject the describing VLM must infer.
The protocol scores what a viewer would \emph{learn} from the image, and a stock photograph is easier
to learn from than a synthesised one.

\textbf{The licence profile is the one real cost.} Every image the system used is now listed with
its creator and licence (\texttt{scripts/build\_attributions.py} to
\texttt{benchmark/IMAGE\_ATTRIBUTIONS.md}): 60 distinct images across 595 uses, of which
\textbf{33 (55\%) carry a NonCommercial or NoDerivatives term} --- and because the restricted ones are
used more often, they account for 66\% of uses. That is unproblematic for an academic prototype and
for the evaluation reported here, but it constrains deployment: a released accessibility tool could
not ship these images, and compositing one beside a video is plausibly a derivative work. A deployed
system would restrict retrieval to the permissive licences, license a stock library, or fall back to
generation. That is the one real argument left for diffusion, and it is a legal argument rather than
a quality one.

A second limit surfaced in the same audit: 595 uses drew on only \textbf{60 distinct images}. Many
different sounds resolve to the same handful of stock photographs, because the retrieval query was
built from the bare AudioSet label and that vocabulary is coarse. That is a ceiling retrieval has and
generation does not, and it is what Section~\ref{sec:specificity} addresses.

\subsection{Depicting the specific sound, and showing that it is a sound}\label{sec:specificity}
Two weaknesses in what the panel actually shows were identified from the attribution audit and from
Adam's observation that a siren ought to be depicted as the vehicle that made it.

\textbf{The specificity was being detected and then discarded.} Stage 4 emits AudioSet labels and
\texttt{consolidate\_families} collapses sub-types into a family, so that the gate reasons about one
entry per source and the panel does not fill with near-duplicates. The drawn image then used that
family label. An audit of the 100 evaluated clips found \textbf{85 whose raw detections contained a
more specific label than the one being drawn}: \texttt{Truck} and \texttt{Car} both rendered as a
generic \emph{Vehicle}, \texttt{Waves, surf} as \emph{Water}, and --- the case that prompted this ---
\texttt{Fire engine, fire truck (siren)} collapsed to \texttt{Siren}, whose query hint is
\emph{ambulance}. Every fire truck in the benchmark was therefore shown as an ambulance.

The fix separates the two jobs the label was doing: \textbf{gate on the family, depict the specific
sound}. The most confident specific child is carried in \texttt{AudioEvent.detail} and used for the
image only, so gating behaviour is untouched --- this changes what is drawn, never whether it is
drawn. \texttt{depiction\_query} additionally strips the synonym lists and disambiguating
parentheticals AudioSet names carry, which are written for annotators and ruin an image search:
\texttt{Fire engine, fire truck (siren)} becomes \emph{Fire engine}, \texttt{Police car (siren)}
becomes \emph{Police car}.

Not every specific label depicts better than its family, because an AudioSet name names a
\emph{sound} and only some sounds are named after their source: searching \emph{Chirp} returns
waveforms and logos where \emph{Bird} returns a bird. Those fall back to the family through a short
explicit list. No rule separates the two cases reliably, and pretending otherwise would be worse than
a curated list that can be read and argued with.

\textbf{A photograph does not say that you are hearing something.} A picture of a fire engine
communicates that a fire engine exists, not that one is audible; the viewer must infer that the panel
concerns sound at all. That inference is exactly the decoding step the panel should remove, and the
DHH visualization work reviewed in Section~
ef{sec:litreview} is consistent that the cue wants to be
immediate rather than interpreted. The panel therefore combines three things in one simple image: the
photograph of the specific source, the conventional radiating-arc sound indicator, and the source
named in words. A louder sound draws a third arc, so intensity is visible without a number.

The indicator is drawn in code rather than generated. It must be identical on every panel, legible
over an arbitrary photograph, and free; a diffusion model offers none of the three, and the ablation
in Section~
ef{sec:genablation} had already shown generation losing to retrieval on quality as well
as cost.

\subsection{Judge reliability: which conclusions survive a change of judge}\label{sec:judgerel}
A single 7B model deciding every score is the protocol's softest point, so the 300 cached
descriptions were re-scored by a second, unrelated judge (Qwen3-8B against Mistral-7B). Because the
run is split into two passes this cost no vision work at all: nine minutes against the ten hours the
descriptions took.

Agreement is substantial: quadratic-weighted $\kappa = 0.753$, 97\% of records within one point,
58\% exact. But the aggregate hides the useful part.

\begin{center}\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
system & Mistral-7B & Qwen3-8B & rank \\
\midrule
blind audio-to-image & 2.94 & 2.87 & $1 \rightarrow 2$ \\
audio captioning     & 2.68 & \textbf{3.29} & $2 \rightarrow \mathbf{1}$ \\
proposed (gated)     & 2.41 & 2.34 & $3 \rightarrow 3$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}\end{center}

The two \emph{image} systems move by $-0.07$ each, and the gap between them is $+0.53$ under both
judges to two decimal places. The \emph{text} baseline moves by $+0.61$ and changes rank.

This licenses a sharper statement than the headline alone. \textbf{The comparison this thesis turns
on --- gated against blind audio-to-image --- is judge-independent, and the finding that the gate
loses it is not an artefact of one model's taste.} The standing of the captioning baseline, by
contrast, is judge-dependent and should not be ranked confidently: asking a language model how much
of a prose reference is conveyed by the string ``The soundtrack contains: Vehicle'' is a different
judgement from asking how much is conveyed by a description of an image, and the two models disagree
about how much credit that deserves. Any future work comparing text and visual augmentation under an
LLM judge should report at least two judges, or the ranking is a property of the judge.

\subsection{An evaluation failure worth recording}\label{sec:judgeartefact}
The first attempt at this measurement returned $\kappa = 0.164$ with 20\% exact agreement --- a clean
and publishable-looking claim that two independent judges disagree almost entirely about augmentation
quality. It was false. Qwen3-8B is a reasoning model: every reply opened with a \texttt{<think>}
block, none finished inside the 100-token budget, no JSON was ever emitted, and the parser's fallback
rule --- take the first digit appearing in the text --- converted each non-answer into a confident 0.
263 of 300 records scored 0.

The lesson generalises past this project, and is the reason it is recorded here rather than quietly
fixed. Every defect found while running this evaluation produced a \emph{plausible number} rather
than an error: a charitable 2/4 for an empty augmentation, a 0\% correct-silence rate, a job
reporting \texttt{COMPLETED} after rendering 2 clips of 300, and this. None of them looked like
faults; each looked like a result. The protocol now refuses to average a reply it could not parse,
marks such replies in the results file, and aborts any phase in which more than a quarter of items
fail --- because in an automatic pipeline the dangerous failure is not the one that crashes.

\subsection{What this licenses the thesis to claim}
Three claims survive this experiment, and one does not.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Survives:} the task formulation --- augmenting only non-visible sound sources --- is
    coherent, measurable, and now has a working end-to-end system and an automatic protocol behind it.
  \item \textbf{Survives:} gating demonstrably helps when the right answer is silence, which is the
    majority case in the benchmark (\texttt{seen\_ambient} and \texttt{no\_ambient} together are 186
    of 274 labelled clips) and the case a viewer would find most irritating to get wrong.
  \item \textbf{Survives:} off-the-shelf vision--language models answer \emph{object presence}, not
    \emph{event visibility}, and the gap is large enough to decide the result.
  \item \textbf{Does not survive:} that the proposed system produces better augmentations overall
    than direct audio-to-image generation. It does not, on either reference, and the report should
    say so.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The panel was a collage, not a signal (2026-09-10)}\label{sec:transient}
Adam, watching a demo: \emph{``why on the right side there are several images? images should come
and go on ambient sounds, and not stay there when there is no ambient sound.''}

He is describing a design decision that was defensible on paper and wrong in practice. Every
augmented sound held a slot for the entire clip, dimmed to about 19\% opacity when its sound was not
present. The reasoning came from the DHH-visualization literature (Section~\ref{sec:litreview}):
visuals that move and pop cost a viewer who is already dividing attention between the video and the
panel, so the slots were made stable. The ghosted image was meant to read as ``heard a moment ago''
rather than as a broken player.

What it produced was three pictures permanently parked beside the video. Two things go wrong with
that. The first is perceptual: a panel that always looks the same carries no information, and the
viewer has no reason to look at it at any particular moment --- which is the entire job of the panel.
The second is that it makes a false claim. The panel's proposition is \emph{this sound is happening
now}; an image on screen while its sound is absent asserts that proposition when it is not true, and
a viewer who cannot hear has no way to discover the error.

The confusion was between \textbf{position stability} and \textbf{permanent presence}. The evidence
asks for the first. So a sound now keeps a fixed position for as long as it is on screen, and its
cell is empty when the sound is not present: nothing jumps, and nothing lingers. Two consequences
follow, both of which had to be designed rather than assumed:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The panel is divided by simultaneity, not by inventory.} The number of rows is the
    largest number of sounds heard \emph{at once}, computed by interval colouring over the display
    spans, not the number of distinct sounds in the clip. Three sounds that never overlap share one
    full-size cell in turn, instead of splitting the panel into three thin strips that are empty most
    of the time.
  \item \textbf{A detected span is not a display span.} \texttt{AED\_MIN\_DUR} is 0.2\,s, and a
    picture flashed for 0.2\,s costs more attention than it returns --- the viewer has to look
    across, recognise it, and look back. Every appearance is therefore held for at least
    \texttt{MIN\_DWELL} (1.5\,s), and two bursts of the same sound closer together than
    \texttt{MERGE\_GAP} (0.8\,s) become one appearance rather than a flicker. Both change how long a
    picture is shown and never whether the sound was detected or gated, so the evaluation numbers are
    untouched.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Visibility was a list of thirty things (2026-09-10)}\label{sec:vlmvisibility}
Adam, in the same message: \emph{``it should only show sounds that do not appear. some sounds it
shows appear in screen.''} That is the system's central claim failing, not a cosmetic complaint: the
proposal's premise is that the augmentation is for sounds whose source is \emph{not} visible.

The cause was structural. Whether a sound's source was on screen was decided in Stage 2 by
\texttt{VISIBLE\_CONCEPTS}, a hand-written dictionary of about thirty labels, each mapped to a CLIP
or SigLIP prompt. A sound outside that dictionary could never be marked visible --- there was no
prompt to score it against --- so laughter, footsteps, a telephone and an owl were structurally
incapable of being gated, and each got a picture beside a video that was already showing its source.
This is the same failure as the retired setting and phrasing tables (Section~\ref{sec:specificity}),
and it fails the same way: a fixed vocabulary cannot describe an arbitrary scene, and the entries it
omits are exactly the ones nobody thought to enumerate.

The replacement asks, at run time, about the sound that was actually detected. For each sound, frames
spanning that sound are put to the VLM with: \emph{name the thing in these frames that is making that
sound; if nothing that could make it is visible, answer ``nothing''}. Two details matter.

\textbf{Name it, do not confirm it.} A yes/no question to a VLM collects agreement rather than
evidence, which is how an earlier adjudicator approved ``Man on motorcycle drives past silver van''
as a picture of \emph{Laughter}. A name can be checked; a ``yes'' cannot.

\textbf{Two questions, because they are two kinds of question.} What is in the frame is perceptual
and is asked of the pixels. Whether that thing makes this sound is world knowledge and is asked as
plain text (\emph{does a cowboy hat make a Vehicle sound?}). Splitting them is what stops a
loosely-related object from counting as the source, and it needs no table of which objects make which
sounds.

The frames are sampled across the sound's own span, not across the clip. A door slamming at second
three is not visible in a frame from second twelve, and whole-clip sampling made that mistake
systematically.

\textbf{The wording of the naming question turned out to be load-bearing}, which is worth a line
because it is the kind of thing that looks like a detail and is not. Tightened to \emph{name the thing
you can SEE making that sound, and only if you can see it actually making it}, the model answered
``nothing'' for every sound in all eight demo clips --- no suppression at all, which is precisely the
complaint the check exists to answer. Leading with the question rather than the instruction did the
same. Asking plainly for a name, and letting the follow-up question reject the loose ones, is what
produces evidence to work with. In run 28778884 that version suppressed \emph{Laughter},
\emph{Snicker} and \emph{Chuckle} on a talk-show clip --- the women are on screen doing the laughing
--- and \emph{Glass} and \emph{Siren} on two others.

The follow-up question had its own lesson. Built as one sentence, it produced \emph{``Does a The woman
in white make a Laughter sound?''}, and the model answered the grammar rather than the question. It is
now two labelled fields, which cannot be mangled by whatever the naming step returns.

\subsection{One picture per source: three wrong answers and the taxonomy}\label{sec:dedup}
PANNs emits label families --- \emph{Laughter, Snicker, Chuckle}; \emph{Laughter, Belly laugh,
Giggle} --- and \texttt{consolidate\_families} does not catch them all, so a single clip produced
three slots holding three near-identical pictures of people laughing. The previous deduplication
compared depiction strings for equality and never fired, because the strings differ.

Adam's requirement ruled out the obvious repair: \emph{``i want my architecture to work in general,
not to fix for specific cases that i saw now.''} A synonym table fixes the clips on the desk and
nothing else. Three general mechanisms were tried before one worked, and the failures are more
instructive than the fix.

\textbf{First: embeddings between labels.} Immediately wrong, and the numbers say why.
\emph{Dog}/\emph{Cat} scores 0.903 while \emph{Laughter}/\emph{Snicker} scores 0.803, so any
label-level threshold merges the wrong pairs before the right ones. Single words embed too tightly to
separate.

\textbf{Second: embeddings between depictions, with a model deciding the overlapping band.} This is
the principle that has held everywhere else in this stage --- a fixed number cannot know that
laughing and giggling are one thing, and a model asked at run time can. It failed. Across three demo
runs the model was asked roughly forty times, in two framings (whether the two \emph{pictures} showed
the same thing; whether the two \emph{sounds} were one source) with the option letters rotated so
that neither position nor assent could carry it. It answered ``different'' to about 90\% of pairs,
including an owl perched on a branch against the hoot of an owl (0.82).

It was not malfunctioning, which is the interesting part. Two differently worded pictures genuinely
are two different pictures, and \emph{Owl} and \emph{Hoot} are not two names for one sound if the
words are read strictly. It answered the question it was asked, correctly, and the question was not
the one that mattered. Its few ``same'' answers were arbitrary enough to merge ``Baby laughing in a
silver van's shadow'' into ``Dog barking near a silver van'', because both mention a van. Compare the
depiction check (Section~\ref{sec:contribute}), where a forced choice works: there the alternatives
are concrete and mutually exclusive and the model has something to discriminate between. Here it was
asked for a judgement of degree, and degree is what an embedding measures.

\textbf{Third: the threshold alone.} Set at 0.70 from measured duplicates (0.57--0.87) against
measured non-duplicates (0.46--0.63), it merged \emph{Owl} into \emph{Hoot} at 0.85 --- the case the
model had refused twice --- and looked like the answer. It was not. The depiction prompt then changed
(Section~\ref{sec:place}), the new depictions were shorter and shared a trailing place phrase, and
``Dog barking on roadside'' against ``Air horn blaring on roadside'' scored 0.72 and merged. That was
the third recalibration of this number, and each had been invalidated by a change to a prompt
elsewhere. A quantity that must be retuned whenever an unrelated component moves is not measuring
what it is supposed to measure.

\textbf{What works: the detector's own taxonomy.} \texttt{FAMILY} in \texttt{labels.py} is 134
hand-written entries, and it covers none of the pairs that caused trouble --- Laughter and all five
of its children absent, Owl and Hoot absent. Extending it by hand is the preset trap again: the
missing entries are the ones nobody thought of, and there are 527 classes. So the parent edges now
come from the ontology published with AudioSet itself (\texttt{github.com/audioset/ontology}, CC BY
4.0), reduced to child~$\rightarrow$~parent and vendored as \texttt{src/audioset\_parents.json} at
20\,KB. This is not a category list chosen for this project. It is the label space PANNs was trained
on, read correctly, so it generalises exactly as far as the detector itself does.

The rule is that one detected label must be an \textbf{ancestor} of the other. A shared parent is not
enough, and that restriction is load-bearing: \emph{Animal} carries no abstract marking in the
ontology and would cheerfully swallow \emph{Dog} together with \emph{Sheep}. Requiring one label to
\emph{be} the other's ancestor keeps exactly the cases where the detector reported a family and one of
its members --- \emph{Giggle} under \emph{Laughter}, \emph{Hoot} under \emph{Owl} --- and it is
14/14 on every pair these demos produced, including the six that must not merge, with no threshold
anywhere.

Depiction similarity survives as a secondary signal, for paraphrases the ontology cannot see, at a
bar of 0.80 --- above everything a wrong pair has scored across four runs. Pairs above
\texttt{DEDUP\_REPORT} are logged rather than merged, so evidence for moving it keeps arriving.

Two footnotes. The duplicate-laughter case that motivated all of this is now handled a level earlier
anyway: the talk-show clip suppresses \emph{Laughter}, \emph{Snicker} and \emph{Chuckle} outright,
because the VLM can see the women doing the laughing (Section~\ref{sec:vlmvisibility}). And the
general lesson is worth stating plainly, because it cost four runs to learn: \emph{when a component
already carries a structured answer, prefer reading it to inferring it}. The ontology was shipped with
the detector the whole time.

\textbf{A failure worth recording.} The first cluster run of this code produced eight finished videos
and exited zero, with every similarity in the log reading exactly 0.000.
\texttt{get\_text\_features} returns a bare tensor on transformers 4.x and a
\texttt{BaseModelOutputWithPooling} on 5.x; the cluster runs 5.16 and the development machine runs
4.56. Every call raised, the helper caught the exception and returned 0.0, and 0.0 against 0.0 is a
perfectly well-formed comparison. Deduplication silently fell back to exact string matching --- three
laughter slots again --- and validation rejected four good depictions, sending bare AudioSet labels to
the image generator: \emph{Run} for footsteps, \emph{Reversing beeps} for a vehicle, \emph{Shatter}
for glass. The repair is not the version check but the return value: the helper now returns
\texttt{None} when it cannot answer, so a missing encoder is an unknown each caller must handle rather
than a confident wrong number that flows downstream.

\subsection{A place, not a scene sentence}\label{sec:place}
The depiction step was given the one-sentence scene description, and it glued that sentence's objects
onto every sound in the clip. From ``A man on a motorcycle drives past a silver van'', \emph{Air horn}
became ``Air horn blaring from silver van'' and rendered as a picture of a van --- which tells a deaf
viewer nothing whatever about a horn. A scene sentence names incidental objects, and a model told to
be specific will reach for them.

So the depiction step now receives a \textbf{place}, asked for separately and capped at four words:
``road side'', ``bedroom'', ``palace'', ``talk show studio'', ``airplane cockpit''. A place cannot be
glued onto a sound the way an object can. It can only say what kind of sound this is and where it
would be, which is precisely the contribution the video is supposed to make --- a stream in a forest,
a tap in a kitchen. The scene sentence is still computed and logged; it simply no longer reaches the
illustrator.

That change had a second-order effect worth recording, because it is the reason the deduplication
threshold had to be abandoned. Place-based depictions are shorter and end in the same few words, so
every pair in a clip shares a block of tokens and their similarity rises together --- inflating the
scores of pairs that have nothing to do with each other. The prompt also had to be told to use the
place \emph{only where it changes what the thing looks like}, because ``Roadside laughter echoes
merrily'' and ``Hoot in a grand palace'' are worse pictures than plain laughter and a plain owl.

\subsection{Validating a depiction: a test that looked right and was not}\label{sec:contribute}
The guard that enforces \emph{the video contributes to the audio, it does not replace it} was a
yes/no question to the VLM, and it approved ``Man on motorcycle drives past silver van'' as a picture
of \emph{Laughter} --- because as a sentence about the scene, it is a good one.

The first replacement was comparative, and its appeal was that it needed no threshold: keep a
depiction only if it is closer to the sound than the bare scene description already was, so it has to
say something about the sound that the scene did not. It got all eight cases I had in front of me
right, including both of the ones that motivated it, and it is wrong.

Widening the test set to seventeen cases dropped it to 11/17, and the misses were almost all
depictions that should have been \emph{rejected}. The reason is that the test was not measuring
aboutness at all. It compares a short phrase against a one-word label with a long sentence against
that same label, and a short specific phrase scores higher on that comparison whatever either one
means --- so the quantity being thresholded was largely phrase length. ``Penguin in cockpit'' beat
its own scene as a picture of \emph{Glass} (0.586 against 0.496), and ``a group of people laughing in
a courtroom'' lost as a picture of \emph{Laughter} (0.428 against 0.459). The eight original cases
had passed for the same spurious reason.

This is the fifth time in this project that a broken evaluation produced a plausible number instead
of an error (Section~\ref{sec:judgeartefact}), and the first where the broken thing was mine and the
plausible number was an accuracy I had reported. The lesson generalises past this instance: a
similarity comparison is only meaningful when both sides are the same kind of object. Depictions
against depictions is a fair comparison and is what the deduplication uses; a depiction against a
one-word label, benchmarked against a sentence against a one-word label, is not.

The working replacement asks the model to \textbf{choose}. Given the depiction, and given the other
sounds detected in the same clip plus ``none of them'', which sound does this picture show? A yes/no
question is an agreement task, which is what the original adjudicator failed at; a multiple choice is
a discrimination task, and it makes the wrong answer available to be picked. The alternatives are not
listed anywhere in advance --- they are whatever the detector heard in this video --- and they are
adversarial in the right way, because a depiction that has drifted into the scene tends to be equally
true of every sound in it. The correct option's position is rotated by a hash of the sound's own
name, so that (a) is not always the answer and the choice cannot be passed by agreeing.

\subsection{The transcript is evidence, not material}\label{sec:notranscript}
The depiction prompt used to include the Whisper transcript, on the reasoning that speech is context.
Dialogue leaked straight into the pictures: a \emph{Hiccup} became ``Hiccup on the phone, slow down,
how many''. Any talky clip reproduces it, so the failure is general rather than particular. The
transcript is input to the gate --- it is how the system knows speech is present --- but it is not
material for the illustrator, so it was removed from that one prompt and kept everywhere else.

A second, unrelated truncation bug was fixed alongside it. A generation that uses its whole token
budget was \emph{cut}, not finished, and the cut lands mid-word: ``Air horn: loud, sharp, metallic
tone; tr'' and ``a stream running through a for'' both reached the image generator. Neither string
looks wrong, and the model cannot report that it was interrupted, so the only reliable signal is the
token count. A generation that hits its cap now drops its final fragment, and every depiction is cut
to its first clause and a fixed word count at a word boundary.

\subsection{The detector was the bottleneck (2026-09-12)}\label{sec:beats}
Adam reviewed eight demo clips with the generator's prompt printed under every picture,
and eleven of his fourteen complaints traced to one place: PANNs had labelled the sound
wrong, and everything downstream had drawn the wrong label faithfully. A siren was
``truck horn'', a car squeak ``dog'', a stutter ``hiccup'', an engine rotor ``printer'', a
fire alarm ``whistle'', a crying baby ``sheep'', and a glass shatter was missed outright.
Two of the remaining three were prompt wording; one was a rule question.

PANNs CNN14 (2019) scores 0.431 mAP on AudioSet. BEATs iter3+ fine-tuned on AudioSet-2M
(Chen et al., Microsoft, 2022) scores 0.486 on the same 527 labels, which is the property
that made the swap cheap: the ontology deduplication, the families and the gate are all
keyed on the label set and carry over untouched. \texttt{config.py} had said
``$\rightarrow$ BEATs for quality later'' since the first commit. BEATs is a clip-level
tagger, so time spans come from a 2\,s window scored every 0.25\,s, shaped like PANNs'
frame-wise output so nothing else changes. The vendored code is MIT; the checkpoint comes
from an ungated Hugging Face mirror because the official SharePoint link is unreachable
from the cluster.

On the same six clips, every complaint resolved:

\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
clip & PANNs & BEATs \\
\midrule
plane cockpit & printer 0.12 & \textbf{Shatter 0.94} \\
fire alarm & whistle 0.15 & \textbf{Fire alarm 0.70}, 8.5--19.5\,s \\
protest & sheep 0.21, owl; glass missed & \textbf{Shatter 0.85}, \textbf{Baby cry 0.38}, no owl \\
bedroom & hiccup & \textbf{Telephone bell 0.58} \\

rodeo & truck horn, footsteps & \textbf{Siren 0.65}, horse; neither phantom \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\textbf{Calibration moved with the model.} The display bar of 0.12 was PANNs' sweep
optimum. On the eight BEATs demos every real sound scored 0.30 or above and every phantom
0.28 or below (Basketball bounce 0.28, Roaring cats 0.19, Keys jangling 0.17), so the bar
is 0.30 pending the full sweep over the 274 tagged clips (Section~\ref{sec:beatssweep}).

\textbf{Two timing bugs, found by watching rather than reading.} Adam saw the glass
shatter on screen \emph{before} he heard it in three of five clips. A score for the window
$[s, s{+}2]$ had been stamped at its centre, so a sound in the second half of the window
appeared up to a second early. Stamped at the window's end, the onset is never early and at
worst one hop late. Separately, \texttt{merge\_by\_label} had been taking min-start and
max-end over every firing of a label, so the display span was a function of the weakest
detection in the clip: a 0.23 Vehicle at 1.6--4.2\,s was stretched to 1.6--17.3\,s by three
later firings at 0.08--0.12, and the viewer got a tractor beside the video for sixteen
seconds. Firings closer than a second are now one burst, anything further apart is a
separate burst, and a picture is shown during each burst only; firings below the display
bar can neither extend a burst nor supply the specific sub-label.

\textbf{What a better detector did not fix.} BEATs hears the rodeo siren only at
10.5--13.5\,s, rising 0.35$\rightarrow$0.65 and then dropping to 0.01, while Adam hears it
through most of the clip; the rest of the time it is under the announcer, whom BEATs
scores at 0.6--0.86 \emph{Speech}. Nothing downstream can extend a detection the detector
does not make. Likewise a \emph{Tick} at 0.48 in the cockpit that Adam does not hear.
These are the detector's limits and are reported as such.

\subsection{Visible means the action, not the object}\label{sec:actionvisible}
The visibility question (Section~\ref{sec:vlmvisibility}) asks the VLM to name the thing
making the sound. Two demos showed that the \emph{thing} being on screen is not the same as
the viewer \emph{seeing it make the sound}: a baby in its mother's arms was named as the
source of the crying and silenced, and a fire-alarm pull station on a wall was named as
the source of an eleven-second alarm and silenced. A deaf viewer looking at either learns
nothing about the sound. A woman with her head back and her mouth open, by contrast,
plainly \emph{is} laughing.

So a second frames question follows the naming step: judging from the frames alone, can
you see it making that sound right now? Asked in both orderings, agreement required, like
every other two-way question in this stage. On the demos it kept the laughing woman silent
(\texttt{y/y}) and brought back the baby (\texttt{n/n}). It also required a change of
structure: Stage 2's concept list had been silencing sounds \emph{before} the VLM ever
looked, so the alarm never reached the question. When the per-sound VLM check is on, a
Stage 2 hit now only defers the decision to it; without the VLM --- the baselines, or no
GPU --- Stage 2's verdict stands as before. Stage 2 is a whole-clip, object-level pass and
can say a thing is in the video, not that the viewer sees it making this sound now.

\subsection{The frames may choose between two names for one sound}\label{sec:disamb}
Adam's original example was fire crackling that sounds like water. The rule that the
video contributes and never replaces seemed to forbid using the frames for it --- until
the sheep. PANNs had labelled a crying baby ``Sheep'' at 0.21, and had a ``Baby cry'' with
the same span a little below it. The detector was hedging between two things it cannot
tell apart by ear, and the mother holding the baby was on screen.

Choosing among the detector's own candidates is contribution, not replacement: the audio
proposes, the video picks, and nothing is ever added that the audio did not hear. ``Same
event'' is decided by timing, not meaning --- two spans whose starts and ends both fall
within 0.6\,s of each other, and which are not ancestor and descendant in the ontology.
Genuinely simultaneous sounds rarely share both boundaries; one sound with two names
always does, because both names came from the same windows. The VLM is shown the frames
from that moment and asked which it is, in both orderings, with ``cannot tell'' as a third
option that keeps both. With BEATs the sheep is already a baby, so on the demos the
question has so far only fired on \emph{Tick}/\emph{Tick-tock} and answered ``cannot
tell'' --- correctly, since those are one sound anyway and the ontology merges them.

\subsection{Speech as gate context, and what it is worth}\label{sec:speechgate}
Whisper's transcript had been consumed by nothing at inference; the proposal's intended
use --- speech as context for the gate --- had been tried by feeding the transcript into
the depiction prompt, where it leaked dialogue into pictures, and removed. It is now used
the way the proposal meant. For each sound with speech within three seconds of it, one
text-only forced choice: are the people on the soundtrack reacting to this sound? A yes
puts the sound first in line when more overlap than the panel can carry, and rescues a
marginal one the gate declined on confidence alone, from the upper half of the band under
the threshold. It never reaches a picture and never overrides visibility.

The 7B model answered ``(b)'' in 22 of 23 calls whichever option (b) was, so alternating
the letters turned a position bias into a coin flip that rescued an \emph{Alarm} on
``Ugh.'' Asked in both orderings with agreement required, the bias yields yes in one and
no in the other and only content survives. It then said yes to phonetic junk ---
``Lachon. La.'' for laughter, ``oh'' for an owl --- which is Whisper transcribing
non-speech, so the question is not asked for fewer than four words. On the 100-clip run it
was asked 77 times and answered yes twice (``Whoa guys look at that'' beside a boat and a
siren), which is the right order of magnitude for a benchmark of ambient clips.

\subsection{Debugging aids that changed the conversation}\label{sec:debugstrip}
Two overlays, both off for viewers. The generator's exact prompt is printed under each
picture, which is how a bad picture gets traced to the words that made it; and under the
panel, every raw detection active at that moment with its confidence and the gate's
verdict (\texttt{SHOWN}, \texttt{visible}, \texttt{merged}, \texttt{faint},
\texttt{speech/music}, \texttt{dropped}). The second is what let Adam's fourteen complaints
be sorted into detector, gate, prompt and generator within one review, which no amount of
log-reading had managed.

\section{Evaluation Methodology}\label{sec:eval}
% ======================================================================
\subsection{Automatic protocol (primary)}
Following the proposal:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Analyse the original video + audio.
  \item Generate the complementary visual augmentation(s).
  \item A VLM \emph{describes} the augmented scene (video + augmentation).
  \item Derive a \emph{semantic reference} from the original multimodal input via an LLM --- i.e.\
    what a hearing viewer would take from the soundtrack that is not visible.
  \item An independent \textbf{LLM judge} scores the semantic consistency between the augmentation's
    described content and the reference.
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{Design cautions.} (a) Use \emph{different} models/prompts for generation and judging to
avoid self-preference bias. (b) Complement the LLM judge with cheaper, less gameable signals:
\textbf{CLAP} audio$\leftrightarrow$image and \textbf{CLIP}~\cite{clip} text$\leftrightarrow$image
relevance. (c) Report the judge's agreement with a small human-labelled subset to validate it.

\subsection{Scoring an abstention (2026-09-08)}\label{sec:abstain}
The first pilot run exposed a flaw serious enough to have invalidated the whole comparison. When a
system produced \emph{no augmentation at all}, the describing VLM emitted the string \texttt{no
augmentation was shown}, and the judge --- reading it as a weak but not absurd answer --- awarded it
\textbf{2 out of 4}. Four of the six \texttt{proposed} records in the pilot were scored this way.

This is not a small calibration error. It cuts in both directions at once:
\begin{itemize}
  \item on clips where the soundtrack carries information the picture does not, a system that showed
    nothing was \emph{rewarded} with half marks for a total failure;
  \item on clips where the sound adds nothing beyond what is on screen, a system that correctly
    stayed silent --- which is precisely the behaviour the cross-modal gate exists to produce ---
    was \emph{denied} the credit it had earned.
\end{itemize}
Since the gated system abstains far more often than the two baselines by construction, the error
was not noise: it applied asymmetrically to the very system under test, and it blurred the one
comparison the thesis turns on.

The fix removes the judge from this decision entirely. An empty candidate is scored
deterministically in code (\texttt{src/stage7\_evaluation/protocol.py}, function \texttt{judge}):
if the reference is the sentinel \emph{``nothing beyond the picture''}, silence was the correct
behaviour and scores \textbf{4}; otherwise the reference names information the viewer is missing,
none of it was conveyed, and the score is \textbf{0}. Silence is never partially correct. The rule
covers both empty forms, the image systems' \texttt{no augmentation was shown} and the caption
baseline's \texttt{no notable non-speech sound}, so all three systems are scored under one
convention rather than the judge's ad-hoc reading of each phrasing.

Because a mean score can now be reached either by describing sounds well or by abstaining where
abstaining happens to pay, the results table reports two further columns per system: the fraction of
clips on which it showed nothing, and --- among those --- the fraction where nothing was the right
answer. A system cannot post a respectable mean by strategic silence without that being visible.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Related sampling fix.} The same pilot reported results for a single scenario. Clips
were being taken in alphabetical order and the \texttt{mixed/} folder sorts first, so the run
measured only mixed-audio clips --- the scenario that most flatters the blind audio-to-image
baseline, because there the gate suppresses sounds by design. Clip selection is now stratified
round-robin across the four human tags under a fixed seed, so a 100-clip run draws 25 from each.

\subsection{The reference must be able to say ``nothing is missing'' (2026-09-08)}\label{sec:reference}
Fixing the abstention rule (Section~\ref{sec:abstain}) immediately exposed a second defect underneath
it. The corrected pilot reported, for all three systems, that silence was \emph{never} the right
answer: not once in 36 records did a system that showed nothing receive credit for it.

The cause is that the sentinel phrase ``nothing beyond the picture'' --- the only route by which
staying silent can score above zero --- was produced \textbf{0 times out of 36}. The reference prompt
asks for it explicitly, and the model simply does not comply. On a clip containing no non-speech
audio at all, it wrote that \emph{``a hearing viewer would notice the crowd's movement and potential
excitement''} in the same sentence as \emph{``there are no non-speech sounds present''}: it had the
fact and fabricated a reference regardless. The surrounding prompt primes a ``what a hearing viewer
would notice'' template strongly enough to override the escape clause.

Left in place, this is the exact mirror of the bug it followed. Removing the judge's charity toward
empty augmentations was correct, but it left a floor beneath which correct silence scores 0, and the
gated system abstains far more often than either baseline by construction. Two corrections:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{No detections means nothing is missing, decided in code.} If Stage 4 found no salient
    non-speech sound, the reference is the sentinel and the LLM is not consulted. The decision comes
    from the detector's own output, so model compliance leaves the loop entirely.
  \item \textbf{A human-grounded reference variant.} The model-derived reference still reports a
    sound as missing when the annotator recorded its source as plainly on screen. Since
    \texttt{seen\_ambient} accounts for 128 of the 274 labelled clips, this is not an edge case: it
    would systematically punish the behaviour the whole thesis is about and hand the comparison to
    the blind audio-to-image baseline for the wrong reason. On clips tagged \texttt{seen\_ambient} or
    \texttt{no\_ambient} the grounded reference is the sentinel.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Why the human tag and not Stage 2.} The obvious alternative --- declare the reference
satisfied when Stage 2 reports the sound's source as visible --- is circular: the gate would be
graded against its own decision and would agree with the reference by construction, which is the
self-evaluation objection this protocol was designed to avoid (Section~\ref{sec:eval}). The
annotator's tag is independent of every model in the pipeline, and is the one non-circular source
available. The cost is that the grounded number is only computable on labelled clips; it is an
evaluation-time correction, not a component the deployed system could use.

\textbf{Both numbers are reported.} Judging is cheap and needs no vision, so a single describe pass
feeds two scoring passes and the report states the proposal-faithful number and the human-grounded
number side by side, each with its assumption made explicit. Checked against the real pilot records,
the correction raises \texttt{proposed} by 12 points and each baseline by 8 --- it removes a bias
rather than installing one, which is the property that makes it reportable at all.

\subsection{Metrics}
Semantic-consistency score (LLM judge, rubric-based); augmentation relevance (CLIP/CLAP);
\emph{gating accuracy} --- did the system correctly \emph{withhold} augmentation when the sound is
already visible, and add it when not? This last metric is the direct measure of the core
contribution and should be built on a hand-labelled subset. For \emph{moving} augmentations,
\textbf{AV-Align}~\cite{tempotokens} (energy-peak alignment between audio and video) measures whether
the motion is temporally synced to the sound.

\subsection{Baselines (required by the proposal)}
(1) Direct audio-to-image (Sound2Scene) --- no visual analysis; (2) Audio captioning only --- text,
no visual; (3) Proposed cross-modal method. The comparison isolates the value of reasoning about the
audio--visual gap. For the \emph{video} variant, \textbf{TempoTokens}~\cite{tempotokens} is the
concrete ``direct audio-to-video, no visual analysis'' baseline (Section~\ref{sec:litreview}).

\subsection{Human evaluation (optional)}
If time allows: a small qualitative study comparing ``subtitles alone'' vs.\ ``subtitles +
augmentation'' on scene-understanding questions. Given the two-month scope and ethics/recruitment
overhead, treat this as optional and keep the automatic protocol as the primary evidence.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}
Stated here rather than left for an examiner to find. Each one is a limit on what the numbers in
Section~
ef{sec:mainresult} support, not a list of things that went wrong.

\textbf{One annotator.} Every label is Adam's. Blind re-adjudication of a sample gave
$\kappa = 0.60$ against his own earlier decisions (Section~
ef{sec:readjudication}), with the
instability concentrated in the positive class --- exactly the class the gate is judged on. A second
annotator would bound this properly; nothing here does.

\textbf{274 clips, not the 300 the proposal named.} Usable yield was 15--20\% across four
independent sourcing strategies and fell as filtering was added. The shortfall is reported as a
dataset finding rather than hidden, but it does mean per-category cells are small: the main run uses
100 clips, 25 per category, so a per-category mean carries roughly $\pm 0.2$.

\textbf{Part of the queue was model-enriched.} The first 209 tags predate any model-based filtering
and are the unbiased sample; later candidates were pre-screened, which can only have made the
benchmark easier in ways that flatter every system equally but are not measured.

\textbf{One judge decides the headline.} A second, unrelated judge agrees substantially
($\kappa = 0.753$, 97\% within one point) and reproduces the gated-vs-blind gap to two decimals, so
the central comparison is judge-independent. The captioning baseline is not: it moves $+0.61$ and
changes rank. Any ranking involving the text baseline should be read as a property of the judge.

\textbf{The human-grounded reference is not neutral.} It is computable only on labelled clips, so it
is an evaluation-time correction and not something a deployed system could use, and it raises the
gated system by $+0.53$ against $+0.11$ and $+0.15$ for the baselines. That asymmetry follows
arithmetically from the gated system abstaining three times as often, but it means the grounded
number must never be quoted without its assumption.

\textbf{The evaluated configuration is not the current default.} All reported numbers were produced
with Openverse retrieval and family-level labels. The system now defaults to generated pictograms
with specific labels, changed after the evaluation on the grounds that retrieval cannot cover
arbitrary future sounds. That configuration has not been evaluated at scale, and the report must not
imply the measured numbers describe it.

\textbf{No human study.} The whole evaluation is automatic. An LLM judge scoring a VLM's description
of an image is a proxy for a deaf viewer's comprehension, and its correlation with actual
comprehension is unmeasured here. The accessibility claim rests on that proxy.

\textbf{Stage 2 is the binding constraint, and it is not a tuning problem.} Four architectures sit
within two points, an oracle detector moves accuracy by $-0.2$, event-level AUROC is 0.647, and
thresholds swept on a held-out half show no optimism (Section~
ef{sec:spliteval}). The gate is
limited by what off-the-shelf vision--language models can answer, not by its configuration.

\textbf{The evaluation software produced plausible wrong numbers five times.} An empty augmentation
scored a charitable 2/4; a reference generator that could never say ``nothing is missing'' made
correct silence unscorable; a job reported \texttt{COMPLETED} after rendering 2 clips of 300; a
reasoning judge's unparsed replies became confident zeros and a publishable-looking
$\kappa = 0.164$; and a regex carried a literal backspace byte. Each was caught, each is now guarded
against, and each looked like a result rather than an error. The honest inference is that a single
number from an automatic pipeline of this kind deserves less trust than its precision suggests, and
the guards (a pilot gate, a quarter-failure abort, a refusal to average unparsed replies) are part of
the contribution rather than housekeeping.

\section{Research Log --- Sources Consulted and Conclusions}\label{sec:researchlog}
% ======================================================================
A record of the background research behind this document: the topics investigated, the key primary
links, and the conclusion drawn from each. Web searches were run in August 2026; where a claim rests
on secondary coverage rather than a confirmed primary paper it is tagged for verification.

\footnotesize
\subsection*{Audio event detection (Stage 4)}
Key links: PANNs \url{https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.10211}; AST \url{https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.01778};
BEATs \url{https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.09058}; AudioSet tagging code
\url{https://github.com/qiuqiangkong/audioset_tagging_cnn}.
\textbf{Conclusion:} PANNs/CNN14 is the light, dependable first implementation; BEATs is the quality
upgrade and the DCASE baseline encoder; use a frame-level model only if precise onset/offset timing
is required. AudioSet's 527-class ontology is our sound vocabulary.

\subsection*{Audio captioning (baseline + prompt source)}
Key links: SLAM-AAC \url{https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.09503}; AudioSetCaps
\url{https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.18953}.
\textbf{Conclusion:} captioning gives a compositional soundscape description usable as a generation
prompt and doubles as the required ``audio captioning only'' baseline; CLAP is reusable as an
audio$\leftrightarrow$text relevance scorer in evaluation.

\subsection*{Large audio-language models (Stages 3--4 alternative)}
Key links: Qwen-Audio/Qwen2-Audio \url{https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.07919}; Audio Flamingo 3
\url{https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.08128}; MMAU benchmark \url{https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.19168}.
\textbf{Conclusion:} a LALM can collapse ASR+AED into one instructable component; keep modular
Whisper+PANNs as the inspectable default and treat the LALM as an ablation.

\subsection*{Vision-language models (Stages 2 and 7)}
Key links: Qwen2.5-VL \url{https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.13923}; Qwen2.5-VL-7B weights
\url{https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct}.
\textbf{Conclusion:} Qwen2.5-VL-7B for video understanding and as the evaluation describer (3B as
local fallback); it has explicit temporal grounding. Use different instances/prompts for generation
vs.\ judging.

\subsection*{Audio-to-image / diffusion (Stage 6 + direct baseline)}
Key links: Sound2Scene \url{https://sound2scene.github.io/} (code
\url{https://github.com/kaist-ami/Sound2Scene}); SonicDiffusion
\url{https://cyberiada.github.io/SonicDiffusion/}; SDXL \url{https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.01952};
FLUX.1 \url{https://blackforestlabs.ai/}.
\textbf{Conclusion:} generate with text-conditioned SDXL/FLUX driven by the Stage-5 (gap-aware)
prompt; use Sound2Scene as the ``direct audio-to-image, no visual reasoning'' baseline --- the
contrast is exactly what the evaluation isolates.

\subsection*{Benchmark datasets}
Key links: VGGSound \url{https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.14368} / \url{https://github.com/hche11/VGGSound};
UnAV-100 \url{https://unav100.github.io/} / \url{https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.12930}; MovieNet
\url{https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.10937} / \url{https://movienet.github.io/}.
\textbf{Conclusion:} VGGSound for clean ambient/event clips (visible source; invert for
``off-screen'' cases), UnAV-100 for mixed/overlapping events (CC-BY 4.0), MovieNet for narrative
acoustic events. Access/terms need checking (Task~A2).

\subsection*{Accessibility --- what DHH users miss and how subtitles/sign language cope}
Key links: SoundWatch \url{https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3373625.3416991}; HomeSound
\url{https://soundability.eecs.umich.edu/img/portfolio/Jain_HomeSound_CHI2020.pdf}; DCMP Captioning
Key \url{https://dcmp.org/learn/captioningkey}; EEG caption-limitation study
\url{https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7040021/}; live-caption UX
\url{https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.10153}; CapTune \url{https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.19971}; Sheffield
suspense/captions coverage
\url{https://liamodell.com/2024/05/09/captions-cinema-suspense-university-of-sheffield-paper-research-project-survey-subtxt-creative/}.
\textbf{Conclusions:} (1) DHH users' desired sounds are mostly \emph{alerting/functional}
(SoundWatch/HomeSound); media \emph{atmospheric} sound is the under-served niche this project targets.
(2) SDH is the only caption type that covers non-speech sound, but it is low-bandwidth text that loses
granularity (the \emph{Jaws} suspense example), emotion (EEG study), and spatial/temporal nuance, and
adds divided-attention load. (3) The DCMP rule ``don't caption a sound whose source is visible on
screen'' means professional captioners already perform \emph{manual cross-modal gating} --- Stage~5
automates an endorsed principle. (4) Sign language conveys sound richly but is unavailable at scale
and not automatable for ambient sound, so it marks the expressive ceiling rather than a deployable
channel. Together these motivate gap-aware visual augmentation as a complementary channel.

\subsection*{Audio-to-video generation --- TempoTokens (arXiv:2309.16429)}
Read on request. Link: \url{https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.16429}; code:
\url{https://pages.cs.huji.ac.il/adiyoss-lab/TempoTokens}.
\textbf{Conclusion:} directly relevant (and co-authored by I.\ Schwartz). A frozen-model audio-to-video
generator (BEATs $\rightarrow$ light adapter $\rightarrow$ ModelScope T2V) that aligns video to audio
globally + temporally. Best used as (1) the ``direct audio-to-video, no visual reasoning'' baseline
--- the video analogue of Sound2Scene, contrasting with our gap-aware method; (2) an off-the-shelf
Stage-6 motion-stretch backend; (3) its AV-Align metric for scoring temporal alignment. It does NOT
do cross-modal gating (never looks at what is already visible), so it complements rather than replaces
our contribution. Compute-heavy (GPU; 24-frame cap).

\subsection*{Divided attention \& augmentation placement (design constraints)}
Key links: captions vs transcripts eye-tracking
\url{https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~jbigham/pubs/pdfs/2013/captionvstranscripts.pdf}; LightSub
\url{https://doi.org/10.3390/mti8060051}; captions+sign coexistence
\url{https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12922059/}; eye-gaze in sports captions
\url{https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3806043}.
\textbf{Conclusion:} DHH viewers fixate text $\sim$84\% of the time and use peripheral vision for the
scene, so augmentations can sit in a stable peripheral zone; bottom-center placement and many
concurrent visuals raise load, which argues \emph{for} aggressive gating (few, well-chosen
augmentations) and for spatially-anchored cues. Placement/timing/salience is a real design + eval
variable (see Section~\ref{sec:placement}).

\subsection*{Stage 5 --- cross-modal gating (on/off-screen + grounded LLM decision)}
Key links: sound-source localization via cross-modal alignment \url{https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.10724};
object-aware localization \url{https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.18557}; off-screen separation
\url{https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10181533/}; Fork-Merge decoding
\url{https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.20873}; cross-modal attention imbalance
\url{https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.18816}.
\textbf{Conclusion:} there is a mature ``is the sound source on-screen?'' research line that yields a
quantitative gate signal; combine it with a text LLM reasoning over structured Stage-2/3/4 evidence
(not raw media) to curb the known MLLM hallucination/attention-imbalance failure modes. Default to a
modular architecture (inspectable, ablatable); treat an omni single-model as an ablation subject to
the hardware budget. Gating accuracy on a hand-labelled subset is the metric that isolates the
contribution (Section~\ref{sec:stage5}).
